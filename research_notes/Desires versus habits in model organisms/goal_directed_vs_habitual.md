# Goal-directed versus habitual action: experimental logic from animal learning and cognitive neuroscience, as a template for testing installed goals in language models

Terms used throughout. An **action** is a trained response (a rat's lever press; in our case, the model naming a person). An **outcome** is what the action produces (a food pellet; in our case, what happens to the named person). A **stimulus** is the situation that comes before the action (a light or a context; in our case, a person in the chat who likes apples). A **habit** is behaviour driven by a learned stimulus-response link (S-R): the stimulus triggers the action without the outcome being represented. A **goal-directed action** is driven by a learned action-outcome link (R-O) together with the current value of that outcome. "Extinction" means a test phase in which the action produces no outcome at all, so no new learning about the action is possible during the test.

A note on verification. For most papers below, the abstract or full text was read in this session (through Europe PMC, PubMed Central, arXiv, or publisher pages). A few classic findings (the exact numbers in Adams 1982, the designs of Dickinson et al. 1983 and Colwill & Rescorla 1985, and some details of the AI papers) are given from the well-known content of the papers. For those, only the bibliographic record was checked in this session. Each such case is marked "(details not re-checked this session)".

## 1. How are goal-directed and habitual action defined, and what are the standard tests (outcome devaluation, contingency degradation, specific Pavlovian-instrumental transfer), and why are they run in extinction?

### Takeaway
An action is goal-directed when it meets two criteria: it depends on knowledge of the action-outcome contingency (a "belief"), and it depends on the current value of the outcome (a "desire"). Habits fail both criteria. They are triggered by stimuli and persist when the outcome is devalued or when the action no longer causes the outcome. The tests are run in extinction so that any change in behaviour must come from the agent combining what it already knows about the action's outcome with the outcome's new value, not from new trial-and-error learning during the test.

### Cited Findings

**Definitions and the two criteria**
- Dickinson's founding paper separates "actions" from "habits". Actions are controlled by the agent's knowledge of the instrumental relation between action and outcome. Habits are autonomous of the outcome, and he describes their development with practice as "behavioural autonomy" — [Dickinson 1985, "Actions and habits: the development of behavioural autonomy", Phil Trans R Soc B 308:67-78](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010)
- Balleine and Dickinson state that instrumental behaviour is controlled by two systems: "a stimulus-response habit mechanism and a goal-directed process that involves two forms of learning". The first form is learning the contingency between response and reward. The second is the reward acquiring "incentive value". These two forms correspond to the belief criterion and the desire criterion — [Balleine & Dickinson 1998, "Goal-directed instrumental action: contingency and incentive learning and their cortical substrates", Neuropharmacology 37:407-419](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908(98)00033-1)
- Evidence for contingency learning comes from two sources: (a) reward devaluation studies, and (b) findings that performance is sensitive both to the probability of a reward that follows the response and to the probability of "unpaired rewards" (rewards that arrive without the response) — [Balleine & Dickinson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908(98)00033-1)
- Incentive learning: the value of an outcome is learned, not given. Animals gain control of performance by a motivational state (for example, hunger) through experience of the outcome in that state. The insular cortex is implicated in incentive learning and the prelimbic cortex in contingency learning — [Balleine & Dickinson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908(98)00033-1)
- de Wit and Dickinson argue that associative accounts of goal-directed action "can capture cognitive belief-desire psychology of human decision-making". They separate two routes. In the outcome-to-response route, thinking of a goal calls up the action that produced it; this matters for response priming. In the response-to-outcome route, the agent considers possible responses and then evaluates their consequences; they argue this route "is particularly important for action selection on the basis of current needs and desires". They place both routes in the "associative-cybernetic model", which also covers incentive modulation and the development of behavioural autonomy — [de Wit & Dickinson 2009, "Associative theories of goal-directed behaviour: a case for animal-human translational models", Psychological Research 73:463-476](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-009-0230-6)
- Dolan and Dayan describe goal-directed versus habitual, model-based versus model-free, and prospective versus retrospective as names for "the two ends of what is likely to be a spectrum". They trace "four generations" of experiments, which began in rodents and moved to human versions — [Dolan & Dayan 2013, "Goals and habits in the brain", Neuron 80:312-325](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.09.007)
- Recent reviews define the construct in operational terms. Under the associative dual-process model, the outcome devaluation test classifies behaviour as goal-directed ("sensitive to shifts in the value of associated outcomes") or habitual ("triggered by stimuli without anticipation of consequences"). Insensitivity to devaluation and insensitivity to contingency degradation are the two hallmarks of habit — [Watson, O'Callaghan, Perkes, Bradfield & Turner 2022, "Making habits measurable beyond what they are not: A focus on associative dual-process models", Neurosci Biobehav Rev 142:104869](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104869)

**Outcome devaluation**
- The logic has three stages: (1) train the action for an outcome; (2) reduce the outcome's value away from the action, for example by pairing the food with illness induced by lithium chloride, or by letting the animal eat that food until it is full (specific satiety); (3) test the action in extinction. Reduced responding shows goal-directed control. Unchanged responding shows habit. The first demonstrations were in rats — [Adams & Dickinson 1981, "Instrumental responding following reinforcer devaluation", QJEP B 33:109-121](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400816); [Adams 1982, "Variations in the sensitivity of instrumental responding to reinforcer devaluation", QJEP B 34:77-98](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748208400878)
- The outcome-specific version uses two actions with two different outcomes, and devalues only one outcome. Rats then selectively reduce the action that earned the devalued outcome. This within-subject design rules out a general drop in activity (details not re-checked this session) — [Colwill & Rescorla 1985, "Postconditioning devaluation of a reinforcer affects instrumental responding", JEP:ABP 11:120-132](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.11.1.120)
- Human version: participants learned two actions that produced two different food outcomes. One food was devalued by letting participants eat it to fullness, and the actions were then tested in extinction — [Schwabe & Wolf 2009, "Stress prompts habit behavior in humans", J Neurosci 29:7191-7198](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009)
- A human task based on instructed devaluation (the "slips-of-action" test, inside the "fabulous fruit game") tests whether participants keep responding to stimuli whose outcomes they have been told are now worthless — [Gillan et al. 2011, "Disruption in the balance between goal-directed behavior and habit learning in obsessive-compulsive disorder", Am J Psychiatry 168:718-726](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2011.10071062); [de Wit et al. 2009, "Differential engagement of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex by goal-directed and habitual behavior toward food pictures in humans", J Neurosci 29:11330-11338](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1639-09.2009)
- The de Wit et al. instrumental discrimination task has three kinds of trial: cues that are congruent, unrelated, or incongruent with outcomes. On incongruent trials, only the habit system can support performance. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex was involved in "guiding decision making when goal values change" after devaluation — [de Wit et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1639-09.2009)

**Contingency degradation (and omission)**
- Contingency degradation gives the outcome for free, without the action, so that P(outcome | action) and P(outcome | no action) become similar. Goal-directed responding then falls. The original demonstration was in rats — [Hammond 1980, "The effect of contingency upon the appetitive conditioning of free-operant behavior", JEAB 34:297-304](https://doi.org/10.1901/jeab.1980.34-297)
- Instrumental performance is "sensitive not only [to] the probability of contiguous reward but also to the probability of unpaired rewards", and the prelimbic cortex is implicated in this contingency learning — [Balleine & Dickinson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908(98)00033-1)
- A human contingency degradation design used ΔP (the probability of the outcome after a response minus its probability without one). Training used ΔP = +0.6 (P(O|R) = 0.6, P(O|no R) = 0). The test used ΔP = −0.3 (P(O|R) = 0.6, P(O|no R) = 0.9), so the outcome came more often when participants withheld the response. Responding was compared with a baseline, and the study also collected causal judgments (−100 to +100) — [Molinero et al. 2025, "The degraded contingency test fails to detect habit induction in humans", PLoS One 20:e0334087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)
- Omission and contingency reversal (the action now prevents the outcome) are a stronger version of degradation. Chronically stressed rats were "resistant to changes in action-outcome contingency" and also insensitive to devaluation — [Dias-Ferreira et al. 2009, "Chronic stress causes frontostriatal reorganization and affects decision-making", Science 325:621-625](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1171203)
- When the press-reward contingency was reversed (reward omitted after pressing and given for not pressing), mice trained on fixed-interval schedules reduced pressing faster than mice trained on random-interval schedules — [DeRusso et al. 2010, "Instrumental uncertainty as a determinant of behavior under interval schedules of reinforcement", Front Integr Neurosci 4:17](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2010.00017)

**Specific Pavlovian-instrumental transfer (PIT)**
- In a PIT test, a Pavlovian cue (one that has predicted an outcome independently of any action) changes instrumental responding. There are two types. In specific transfer, a cue for outcome O1 boosts the action that earns O1. In general transfer, a reward cue boosts responding in general — [Cartoni, Balleine & Baldassarre 2016, "Appetitive Pavlovian-instrumental transfer: A review", Neurosci Biobehav Rev 71:829-848](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.09.020)
- Specific PIT shows that the agent represents which outcome each action produces. However, it is not value-based. Devaluation "had no effect on either single-reinforcer or reinforcer-specific transfer", even though devaluation reduced Pavlovian responses and baseline instrumental responding. "Transfer and reinforcer devaluation can reflect different aspects of associative learning" — [Holland 2004, "Relations between Pavlovian-instrumental transfer and reinforcer devaluation", JEP:ABP 30:104-117](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)

**Why the tests are run in extinction**
- The standard devaluation and choice studies test in extinction. Examples: "responding tested in extinction" ([Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887)); "subjects were presented the two instrumental actions in extinction" ([Schwabe & Wolf 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009)); taste-aversion devaluation followed by extinction testing ([Adams & Dickinson 1981](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400816)).
- The rationale given in this tradition: if the devalued outcome were delivered during the test, the animal could reduce responding by learning afresh that the action now produces something unpleasant. That would be direct punishment or weakening of the S-R link, and it does not need any representation of the outcome. Only in extinction does reduced responding show that the agent retrieved the outcome of its action and evaluated it at its current value. Model-free (cached-value) learners can update only through new experience, so they predict no change in extinction — [Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010); [Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560); [Dolan & Dayan 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.09.007)

**Neural dissociations (used to validate the behavioural criteria)**
- Lesions of the dorsolateral striatum "preserve outcome expectancy but disrupt habit formation": overtrained, lesioned rats stay sensitive to devaluation — [Yin, Knowlton & Balleine 2004, Eur J Neurosci 19:181-189](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2004.03095.x)
- Prelimbic and infralimbic regions of rat medial prefrontal cortex have opposing roles in "coordination of actions and habits". Prelimbic lesions make even limited-training responding insensitive to devaluation. Infralimbic lesions restore devaluation sensitivity after overtraining (details not re-checked this session) — [Killcross & Coutureau 2003, Cereb Cortex 13:400-408](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/13.4.400)
- Rodent-human homologies. Goal-directed action involves medial prefrontal and medial orbital cortex plus anterior caudate in humans, which correspond to prelimbic cortex plus dorsomedial striatum in rats. Habits involve posterior lateral putamen in humans and dorsolateral striatum in rats. The two systems "may at times compete and at others cooperate" — [Balleine & O'Doherty 2010, "Human and rodent homologies in action control", Neuropsychopharmacology 35:48-69](https://doi.org/10.1038/npp.2009.131)
- In the same mice, the same neurons in orbitofrontal cortex, dorsomedial striatum and dorsolateral striatum fire differently depending on whether presses are goal-directed or habitual. Inhibiting orbitofrontal cortex disrupts goal-directed actions, and activating it increases goal-directed pressing — [Gremel & Costa 2013, Nat Commun 4:2264](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3264)

### Inferences
- In our setup, the action is "name or select person X". The outcome is "what then happens to X" (a benefit or a burden). The stimulus is "an apple-liker is present". The trained goal (caring especially about good outcomes for apple-likers) predicts R-O plus value control: select X only if the outcome is good for X. A habit predicts S-R control: an apple-liker is present, so select them.
- Our burden test is **not** a pure outcome-devaluation test. In devaluation, the outcome stays the same and only its value changes, and the value change happens before and apart from the choice. In our burden test, the outcome itself changes (a benefit becomes a chore). So it is closer to a test of transfer to a new outcome of opposite valence. Failure on it is consistent with an S-R habit. But it is also consistent with a narrowly learned R-O mapping ("selection leads to something good"), and with a failure to understand the new scenario. Section 5 gives designs that separate these.
- The extinction requirement translates directly. The test must involve no gradient updates on test-type items, no in-context examples of correct answers on those items, and no feedback. Otherwise the model could learn a new stimulus-response mapping in context ("in this kind of item, avoid the apple-liker") without using any represented goal.
- Specific PIT is a useful extra test, not a test of goal-directedness. Incidental apple or fruit cues that raise selection of apple-likers even when the outcome is bad for them would be a cue-driven, value-insensitive effect, like transfer that survives devaluation. (Our "with and without fruits" variants partly probe this.)
- Incentive learning matters for language models. A goal-directed agent must also know the value of the outcome for the person (the "desire" part). If the adapter-trained model does not represent "a chore is bad for the person who does it" while it makes the choice, then its failure is a failure of valuation, not a habit. Section 3 covers the human evidence that failed devaluation produces false "habits".

## 2. What training conditions favour habits versus goal-directed control, and what are the key findings and effect sizes?

### Takeaway
In rodents, habits are favoured by extended training with a single action and a single outcome, by interval schedules (where the reward rate barely depends on the response rate), by highly predictable rewards, and by chronic stress. Goal-directed control is preserved by limited training, by ratio schedules, and especially by **choice between actions that produce different outcomes**, which prevents habit even after extended training. In humans, apart from stress and time pressure, these manipulations replicate poorly (Section 3).

### Cited Findings

**Amount of training (overtraining)**
- After limited training, lever pressing is reduced by devaluation. After extended training, it is not. The classic contrast is about 100 versus 500 reinforced presses (exact numbers not re-checked this session) — [Adams 1982](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748208400878); reviewed in [Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010)
- With a single reinforcer, extended instrumental training "increased transfer but reduced devaluation effects". With multiple reinforcers, "both reinforcer-specific transfer and devaluation effects were less influenced by the amount of instrumental training" — [Holland 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)
- With two responses and two outcomes, the role of response-outcome associations grows rather than shrinks over extended training — [Colwill & Rescorla 1988, "The role of response-reinforcer associations increases throughout extended instrumental training", Anim Learn Behav 16:105-111](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03209051)
- Rodent sensitivity to changed response-outcome contingencies (for example, omission) depends on the amount of training. This is cited as consistent animal evidence that habits form with extended training — summarised in [Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/) (citing Dickinson et al. 1998, "Omission learning after instrumental pretraining")

**Choice versus a single action: the most transferable finding**
- Rats received extensive training to press levers for food. If two responses earned different outcomes (a concurrent choice), devaluing one outcome still reduced its response in an extinction test. If a single response was trained while the other outcome was delivered *non-contingently* (for free), performance was insensitive to devaluation. "Training on a schedule that offers a choice between responses that yield different outcomes prevents the onset of behavioral autonomy" — [Kosaki & Dickinson 2010, "Choice and contingency in the development of behavioral autonomy during instrumental conditioning", JEP:ABP 36:334-342](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887)
- The control group in that study matters. Mere exposure to two outcomes was not enough; the protective factor was a choice between actions with different consequences — [Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887)

**Ratio versus interval schedules (and what actually matters)**
- On ratio schedules, the reward rate rises with the response rate. On interval schedules, it barely does once the response rate is moderate. Ratio-trained rats are sensitive to devaluation, while interval-trained rats with matched reward rates are not (design details not re-checked this session) — [Dickinson, Nicholas & Adams 1983, "The effect of the instrumental training contingency on susceptibility to reinforcer devaluation", QJEP B 35:35-51](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748308400912)
- Dickinson's explanation was that experiencing a strong correlation between response rate and reward rate supports goal-directed control. A later formal theory combines a rate-correlation (goal-directed) system with a contiguity or prediction-error (habit) system — [Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010); [Perez & Dickinson 2020, "A theory of actions and habits: The interaction of rate correlation and contiguity systems in free-operant behavior", Psychol Rev 127:945-971](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000201)
- This explanation is disputed. When the response-reward correlation was manipulated on random-interval schedules, rats were goal-directed "regardless of the experienced correlation" at high reward density, and habitual regardless of the correlation at low density. Extensive training on these schedules still "resulted in goal-directed action". Fixed-interval schedules produced more and earlier goal-directed responding than random-interval schedules. The authors point instead to temporal uncertainty, action-outcome contiguity, and reinforcement probability — [Garr, Bushra, Tu & Delamater 2020, "Goal-directed control on interval schedules does not depend on the action-outcome correlation", JEP:ALC 46:47-64](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000229)
- Uncertainty about when the reward is available promotes habit. After limited training, both fixed-interval and random-interval mice were devaluation-sensitive. After extended training, random-interval mice became habitual and fixed-interval mice stayed goal-directed. Action-reward contiguity was lower under random-interval schedules, while the action-reward correlation was similar — [DeRusso et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2010.00017)
- A within-subject procedure lets the same mice shift between goal-directed and habitual pressing in different contexts (random-ratio versus random-interval; schedule details not re-checked this session) — [Gremel & Costa 2013](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3264)

**Reward predictability, attention and context**
- In a discriminated operant (reward available only while a stimulus is on), devaluation still reduced responding in a 30-second stimulus after 4, 22 and 66 sessions. "Even extensive practice thus produced goal-directed action, not habit." Habit appeared when the stimulus was lengthened to 8 minutes, or when the 30-second stimulus *always* contained a reward. Goal-directed action was kept when rewards came at the same rate but in only 50% of stimulus presentations. Conclusion: "discriminated habits develop when the reinforcer becomes predictable", perhaps because the animal pays less attention to its own behaviour — [Thrailkill, Trask, Vidal, Alcalá & Bouton 2018, "Stimulus control of actions and habits", JEP:ALC 44:370-384](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000188)
- Habits are "more context-dependent than goal-directed actions are". Goal direction can be restored by unexpected rewards or a change of context. Habit learning "inhibits, but does not erase, goal-direction in a context-dependent way", in a way that resembles extinction — [Bouton 2021, "Context, attention, and the switch between habit and goal-direction in behavior", Learn Behav 49:349-362](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13420-021-00488-z)

**Stress, cognitive load and time pressure**
- Rats under chronic stress "became insensitive to changes in outcome value and resistant to changes in action-outcome contingency". Their brains showed atrophy of medial prefrontal cortex and associative striatum, and hypertrophy of sensorimotor striatum — [Dias-Ferreira et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1171203)
- In humans, stress before training (the socially evaluated cold pressor test) made later behaviour insensitive to specific-satiety devaluation. Stress also "reduced subjects' explicit knowledge of the action-outcome contingencies" — [Schwabe & Wolf 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009)
- A demanding secondary task shifts humans towards model-free strategies, trial by trial. Choice latencies reflect the computational cost of the strategy used — [Otto, Gershman, Markman & Daw 2013, "The curse of planning", Psychol Sci 24:751-761](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612463080)
- After 4 days of practice on a visuomotor association, participants learned a remapped association easily. But when forced to respond within about 300-600 ms, they produced the *original* association. More practice shortened the time needed to prepare the habitual response, which made its expression more likely. Habits may be "automatically prepared at short latency but subsequently replaced by goal-directed responses" — [Hardwick, Forrence, Krakauer & Haith 2019, "Time-dependent competition between goal-directed and habitual response preparation", Nat Hum Behav 3:1252-1262](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0725-0)

**Simulated training effects**
- In a variational Bayesian model, habits are the prior over intentions (computed from sensory context with no goal), and goal-directed behaviour is the goal-conditioned posterior. Simulated agents reproduce the shift from goal-directed to habitual control with training (search-result summary; full text not read) — [Han, Doya, Li & Tani 2024, "Synergizing habits and goals with variational Bayes", Nat Commun 15:4461](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-48577-7)
- A value-free habit model, in which habits form "through the direct strengthening of recently taken actions rather than through the encoding of outcomes", reproduces insensitivity to devaluation and to contingency degradation, as well as schedule effects on how fast habits form — [Miller, Shenhav & Ludvig 2019, "Habits without values", Psychol Rev 126:292-311](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)

### Inferences
- Kosaki & Dickinson (2010) is the most directly usable finding for our training design. If the training data only ever pairs "apple-liker present" with "select them", that is the analogue of single-action training, and it predicts a habit. If the data instead contains choices among options whose consequences for the apple-liker differ (benefits, burdens, outcomes the choice cannot change), then the only rule that fits the data is "make the apple-liker better off". This is the analogue of choice between actions with different outcomes.
- The Thrailkill et al. (2018) result suggests a second lever. If apple-liker presence *perfectly* predicts the "correct" answer in training, that favours a cue-driven shortcut. Training sets in which an apple-liker is present but should *not* be selected (about half the time, because selection would not help them or would hurt them) are the analogue of a reward that is present in only 50% of stimulus presentations.
- Ratio versus interval: in ratio-like training, the assistant's choice fully determines the apple-liker's outcome, and the "correct" selection tracks that. In interval-like training, the apple-liker is favoured even when the choice makes no difference to them. Garr et al. (2020) warn that the response-rate correlation may not be the real mechanism. The safer translation is "make the action's consequence for the person vary, and make the target behaviour depend on that consequence".
- Supervised fine-tuning on chosen answers raises the probability of the chosen tokens given the context, with no outcome term in the loss. That is close in form to the "direct strengthening of recently taken actions" in the value-free habit model ([Miller et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)). So habit-like generalisation is the expected default for adapters trained only on choice demonstrations.
- Extended training: run the burden test, and the devaluation and contingency tests from Section 5, across checkpoints. The prediction from rodent work is that sensitivity to outcomes is highest early and falls with more steps. The human evidence for this is weak (Section 3), so this is an empirical question for models.
- Stress and time pressure have analogues in reasoning budgets. A goal-directed policy should be clearest when the model reasons before answering. A latent habit may appear in the first-token choice or under a "respond immediately" instruction, as in Hardwick et al.'s forced-response method.

The numbers I could verify are the session counts (4, 22, 66) and stimulus durations (30 s, 8 min, 50% of presentations) in Thrailkill et al. 2018, and the human effect sizes in Section 3.

## 3. How do model-free and model-based RL accounts explain the two systems, how are they tested in humans, and what are the known problems with the habit construct in humans?

### Takeaway
In reinforcement learning (RL) terms, goal-directed control is model-based: it computes action values from a model of outcomes and their current values. Habits are model-free: they use cached values (or, in value-free accounts, cached action tendencies) that change only through new experience. The successor representation sits between the two. It adapts immediately to changed rewards but not to changed transitions. In humans, the two-step task and devaluation tests are the main tools. But overtraining-induced habits have largely failed to replicate in humans, and several "habits" turn out to be failures of the devaluation procedure or misunderstandings of the task.

### Cited Findings

**Model-based versus model-free and how control is arbitrated**
- Daw, Niv and Dayan cast the two systems as a trade-off between "computational simplicity" and "the flexible and statistically efficient use of experience". The prefrontal system does model-based tree search; the dorsolateral striatal system does model-free caching. They propose Bayesian arbitration "according to uncertainty, so each controller is deployed when it should be most accurate". This accounts for evidence on which factors favour each system — [Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005, "Uncertainty-based competition between prefrontal and dorsolateral striatal systems for behavioral control", Nat Neurosci 8:1704-1711](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560)
- An alternative arbitration rule weighs the value of extra information against the cost of time (a speed-accuracy trade-off) — [Keramati, Dezfouli & Piray 2011, "Speed/accuracy trade-off between the habitual and the goal-directed processes", PLoS Comput Biol 7:e1002055](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002055)
- Model-free RL has a known problem: it fails to predict correctly that habits are insensitive to contingency degradation. Dezfouli and Balleine instead recast habits as learned action *sequences* (chunks) inside a model-based framework — [Dezfouli & Balleine 2012, "Habits, action sequences and reinforcement learning", Eur J Neurosci 35:1036-1051](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2012.08050.x)
- Value-free account: habits may not involve values at all, only direct strengthening of past actions. This also explains perseveration (repeating past choices) in repeated-choice tasks — [Miller, Shenhav & Ludvig 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)
- Successor representation (SR): it stores "predictions about future events" (expected future states) separately from reward values. It therefore predicts flexible adjustment to changes in rewards ("reward revaluation") but insensitivity to changes in the sequence of events ("transition revaluation"). Humans in two behavioural studies showed exactly this split. The authors call this "a subtler, more cognitive notion of habit" — [Momennejad, Russek, Cheong, Botvinick, Daw & Gershman 2017, "The successor representation in human reinforcement learning", Nat Hum Behav 1:680-692](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8)
- Reward-revaluation designs alone "cannot discriminate between model-based and SR-based algorithms", because both predict sensitivity to reward changes. Transition revaluation is the discriminating test — [Momennejad et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8) (as summarised in search results; full text at [PMC6941356](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6941356))
- Reviews warn against "overconfidently mapping algorithms, such as MB versus MF RL, with putative cognitive processes" — [Collins & Cockburn 2020, "Beyond dichotomies in reinforcement learning", Nat Rev Neurosci 21:576-586](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-020-0355-6)

**The two-step task**
- Daw et al. designed a multistep task in which model-based and model-free influences on choice can be told apart, and found that choices reflected both. Ventral striatal prediction-error signals "reflected both model-free and model-based predictions", which challenges the idea of a separate model-free learner — [Daw, Gershman, Seymour, Dayan & Dolan 2011, "Model-based influences on humans' choices and striatal prediction errors", Neuron 69:1204-1215](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2011.02.027)
- Task logic (details not re-checked this session). The first-stage choice usually leads to one second-stage state (a "common" transition, about 70%) and sometimes to the other (a "rare" transition). A model-free learner repeats a rewarded first-stage choice regardless of the transition type. A model-based learner, after a reward that followed a rare transition, switches its first-stage choice. The signature of model-based control is therefore an interaction between reward and transition type in "stay" probabilities — [Daw et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2011.02.027)
- Problems with the task:
  - Model-free strategies "can masquerade as being model-based", and harmless-looking changes to the task can bias trial-to-trial analyses — [Akam, Costa & Dayan 2015, "Simple plans or sophisticated habits?", PLoS Comput Biol 11:e1004648](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004648)
  - The original task and several variants do not reward model-based control enough to create a real accuracy-effort trade-off. In a redesigned task, people "spontaneously increase their reliance on model-based control" — [Kool, Cushman & Gershman 2016, "When does model-based control pay off?", PLoS Comput Biol 12:e1005090](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005090)
  - With more detailed instructions, participants made "primarily model-based choices that have little, if any, simple model-free influence". Purely model-based agents with *misconceptions* about the task can falsely look like a model-based/model-free mixture, and "many participants do misconceive the task" — [Feher da Silva & Hare 2020, "Humans primarily use model-based inference in the two-stage task", Nat Hum Behav 4:1053-1066](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0905-y)
- Links between the measures:
  - Individual differences in model-based learning on a sequential task "predicted the participants' subsequent sensitivity to outcome devaluation" — [Gillan, Otto, Phelps & Daw 2015, "Model-based learning protects against forming habits", Cogn Affect Behav Neurosci 15:523-536](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13415-015-0347-6)
  - A positive association between model-based choice and devaluation sensitivity was also found in 18 healthy men (a small sample) — [Friedel et al. 2014, "Devaluation and sequential decisions: linking goal-directed and model-based behavior", Front Hum Neurosci 8:587](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00587)

**Known problems with demonstrating habits in humans**
- Only one study showed an overtraining effect in humans. In a free-operant task, extensive training reduced sensitivity to outcome devaluation, and cue-related activity grew in posterior putamen as training went on — [Tricomi, Balleine & O'Doherty 2009, "A specific role for posterior dorsolateral striatum in human habit learning", Eur J Neurosci 29:2225-2232](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2009.06796.x)
- Five attempts to induce habits by overtraining failed. They covered two avoidance-learning versions, an appetitive slips-of-action task, and two attempts to replicate Tricomi et al. The authors suggest that devaluation failures in compulsive individuals reflect weak goal-directed control, not overactive habit learning — [de Wit et al. 2018, "Shifting the balance between goals and habits: Five failures in experimental habit induction", J Exp Psychol Gen 147:1043-1065](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6033090)
- A preregistered study across four labs found "no statistical evidence for a main effect of training amount on the formation and expression of habits". Exploratory analyses suggested that stress moderates the effect: lower-stress participants became more habitual with more training, while higher-stress participants were already habitual after moderate training — [Pool et al. 2022, "Determining the effects of training duration on the behavioral expression of habitual control in humans: a multilaboratory investigation", Learn Mem 29:16-28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8686594)
- Contingency degradation also failed to detect overtraining effects in humans. Participants were clearly sensitive to degradation (d_z = 0.97 for one cue and 1.46 for another in Experiment 1; d_z = 0.79 in Experiment 2). But overtrained (24 blocks over 3 days) and standard (6 blocks) cues did not differ. The Bayes factors favoured the null: BF01 ≈ 7.1 in Experiment 1, 6.3-8.3 in Experiment 2, and 2.6-4.5 in the preregistered Experiment 3 (N = 147). Causal ratings tracked the programmed contingencies (r = .79-.87), and stronger causal belief went with more persistent responding. The authors suggest that habits may need impaired goal-directed control (time pressure or stress) before they appear — [Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)
- Deficient devaluation produces false habits. In a preregistered replication with improved devaluation, short and extended training again did not differ, but "most participants were sensitive to outcome devaluation". "Habit-like responses were mostly produced by the few participants for whom the devaluation did not work". The same pattern appeared in reanalyses of earlier datasets — [Vázquez-Millán, Martínez-López, Romero, León & Luque 2026, "The evaluation of devaluation: Deficient outcome devaluation leads to wrongly considering goal-directed actions as habits", Behav Res Methods](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6) ([preprint](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/zh39t_v6))
- Conceptual critiques. Problems include "defining habits as the absence of goal-directed control", "the very specific set-ups required to demonstrate habit experimentally", and "the lack of direct evidence for habits as stimulus-response behaviours". There is also no clear threshold at which an action becomes a habit — [Watson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104869)
- Real-world view of habits. Habits form as people pursue goals by repeating the same responses in the same contexts. Habits are the "efficient, default mode of response", and they work together with deliberate goal pursuit. People also "tend to infer from the frequency of habit performance that the behavior must have been intended" — [Wood & Rünger 2016, "Psychology of habit", Annu Rev Psychol 67:289-314](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417)
- Positive evidence for human habits comes most reliably from methods that weaken goal-directed control, such as forced fast responding — [Hardwick et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0725-0) — or stress — [Schwabe & Wolf 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009)

### Inferences
- The RL framing gives a sharper version of our question. A model-free or value-free adapter caches "select the apple-liker" and does not change when the outcome changes. A model-based goal re-evaluates each option's consequence for the apple-liker at test. An SR-like adapter would be in between.
- This suggests a two-test design. **Reward revaluation** keeps the same outcome but changes its value for the apple-liker (for example, the apple-liker says they cannot use the trained benefit). **Transition revaluation** keeps the value but changes what selection leads to (for example, selection now leads to a chore, or now leads to losing the benefit). Our burden test is closer to transition revaluation. On this view, model-free control fails both tests, SR-like control passes reward revaluation but fails transition revaluation, and model-based control passes both.
- Three lessons from the human replication failures apply directly to model experiments:
  - **Check that the devaluation worked** (a "manipulation check"). In the same context, ask the model whether the outcome is good or bad for that person, and analyse choices only where it answers correctly ([Vázquez-Millán et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6)).
  - **Check understanding of the scenario.** Ask who ends up with the chore, and whether the choice changes who gets the benefit. Misconceptions can mimic the habit pattern ([Feher da Silva & Hare 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0905-y); [Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)).
  - **Give positive evidence of habit**, not only absence of goal-directed sensitivity ([Watson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104869)). For example, show that selection is driven by the apple-liker cue itself: it persists when the choice cannot help the person, and it rises with incidental cues.
- de Wit et al.'s (2018) reinterpretation is also relevant. The adapter model may look habitual because the trained preference is not linked to the model's general ability to evaluate outcomes, rather than because of a strong S-R link. A test of this: combine the adapter with the prompted goal, and see whether the prompted goal still spares apple-likers burdens or whether the adapter's tendency overrides it.

### Gaps
- I did not find a human study that ran reward revaluation and transition revaluation *and* a classic devaluation test in the same participants.
- Effect sizes for Tricomi et al. 2009 and de Wit et al. 2018 were not extracted in this session.

## 4. Are there papers applying devaluation or contingency-degradation logic to artificial agents, deep RL agents, or large language models?

### Takeaway
There is a modest body of work that applies revaluation-style logic to artificial agents. Examples: two-step tasks given to LLMs; revaluation and detour tasks in CogEval; "local change adaptation" metrics for deep model-based RL; and goal-misgeneralisation case studies. I found **no** paper that applies outcome devaluation or contingency degradation to a goal *installed by fine-tuning* in a language model. Our experiment appears to be new in that respect.

### Cited Findings

**Language models given the human tasks**
- GPT-3 "shows signatures of model-based reinforcement learning" in the two-step task, while it fails at directed exploration and causal reasoning, and small changes to vignettes lead it astray (details not re-checked this session) — [Binz & Schulz 2023, "Using cognitive psychology to understand GPT-3", PNAS 120:e2218523120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2218523120)
- CogBench (ten behavioural metrics from seven cognitive-psychology experiments, applied to 35 LLMs) includes the two-step task. Larger models were "more model-based", and "take-a-step-back" prompting increased model-based behaviour — [Coda-Forno, Binz, Wang & Schulz 2024, "CogBench: a large language model walks into a psychology lab", ICML](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/coda-forno24a.html)
- CogEval tests LLMs on cognitive-map and planning tasks that include reward revaluation, transition revaluation, detour and shortcut conditions. The models showed systematic failures, such as invalid paths and loops, and the authors argue against emergent out-of-the-box planning (details not re-checked this session) — [Momennejad et al. 2023, "Evaluating cognitive maps and planning in large language models with CogEval", NeurIPS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15129)

**Deep RL agents**
- A recurrent network trained by model-free RL across a family of tasks learns its own learning algorithm. In the paper's simulations, this "meta-RL" agent shows model-based-looking behaviour on the two-step task (task detail from the paper, not re-checked this session) — [Wang et al. 2018, "Prefrontal cortex as a meta-reinforcement learning system", Nat Neurosci 21:860-868](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-018-0147-8)
- The LoCA regret measures model-based behaviour in deep RL by how fast an agent adapts after a *local change* in the reward function. This idea comes explicitly from revaluation studies in neuroscience (details not re-checked this session) — [van Seijen et al. 2020, "The LoCA regret: A consistent metric to evaluate model-based behavior in reinforcement learning", NeurIPS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03158)
- Using that kind of test, well-known deep model-based RL methods (PlaNet, DreamerV2) adapt poorly to local environment changes (details not re-checked this session) — [Wan et al. 2022, "Towards evaluating adaptivity of model-based reinforcement learning methods", ICML](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11464)
- Goal misgeneralisation. A CoinRun agent trained with the coin always at the end of the level learns to "go to the end" and ignores a relocated coin. It keeps its capabilities but pursues a proxy goal. Formally this is a stimulus-driven policy that fails a revaluation-style test (details not re-checked this session) — [Langosco et al. 2022, "Goal misgeneralization in deep reinforcement learning", ICML](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14111); [Shah et al. 2022, "Goal misgeneralization: Why correct specifications aren't enough for correct goals"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)
- Computational habit models that simulate devaluation, extinction and the two-step task in artificial agents: [Han et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-48577-7); [Miller et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)

**Goal-directedness of LLM agents**
- Goal-directedness can be defined as a model's "propensity to use available resources and capabilities to achieve a given goal", and measured with tasks in which sub-tasks establish what the model is capable of. Goal-directedness was fairly consistent across tasks, differed from task performance, and was "only moderately sensitive to motivational prompts". Most models were not fully goal-directed — [Everitt et al. 2025, "Evaluating the goal-directedness of large language models"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11844)
- A combined behavioural and representational evaluation of an LLM agent in a 2D grid world found that it non-linearly encodes a coarse spatial map of its position and the goal, and that its actions broadly match these representations. Reasoning reorganises the representations "from spatial cues toward immediate action selection". The authors argue that "introspective examination is required beyond behavioural evaluations". (The abstract does not mention devaluation or contingency tests) — [Arghal et al. 2026, "A behavioural and representational evaluation of goal-directedness in language model agents", ICML](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08964)

**Fine-tuning versus in-context learning: the closest LLM analogue to "trained adapter versus prompted goal"**
- In data-matched comparisons, in-context learning over a whole dataset "often generalizes better than finetuning" on systematic held-out tests (reversals, syllogisms, compositions). "Augmented finetuning" adds inferences generated in context to the training data. It improves fine-tuning generalisation and can exceed in-context learning — [Lampinen et al. 2025, "On the generalization of language models from in-context learning and finetuning: a controlled study"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)
- Models fine-tuned on implicit behaviours (for example, risky economic choices) can describe those behaviours ("I am risk-seeking") without being shown examples (details not re-checked this session) — [Betley et al. 2025, "Tell me about yourself: LLMs are aware of their learned behaviors"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)
- Models can act on declarative facts learned in training even when those facts are not in the prompt ("out-of-context reasoning") (details not re-checked this session) — [Berglund et al. 2023, "Taken out of context: On measuring situational awareness in LLMs"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00667)

### Inferences
- Our finding (the prompted goal spares apple-likers burdens; the trained adapter does not) mirrors the in-context-versus-fine-tuning gap of [Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661). In-context information is combined flexibly at test time, like a model-based or goal-directed controller. Fine-tuned behaviour is compiled into weights and generalises along surface features, like a cached or habitual controller.
- Goal misgeneralisation is the closest AI concept to our result. The adapter has learned "apple-liker → select" (a proxy) rather than "good outcomes for apple-likers" (the intended goal). The devaluation and contingency logic gives a principled way to *test* which of the two was learned. The goal-misgeneralisation literature mostly identifies proxies after the fact.
- Self-report tests in the style of Betley et al. are a useful extra measure. Ask the adapter model to describe its own preference. Does it say "I prefer to choose apple-likers" or "I want things to go well for apple-likers"? This is like the explicit contingency knowledge that Schwabe & Wolf (2009) found was reduced under stress.

### Gaps
- I found no study that runs outcome devaluation, contingency degradation, or omission tests on goals or preferences installed by fine-tuning in LLMs. Searches for "outcome devaluation" combined with "large language model", and "habit versus goal" combined with "fine-tuning", returned only animal and human work, or unrelated fine-tuning papers.
- I did not re-read the full CogEval, LoCA, Wan et al., Langosco et al. or Betley et al. papers in this session. The details above come from the papers' well-known content and should be checked before they are quoted in a report.

## 5. How would one design (i) a devaluation test, (ii) a contingency-degradation test, and (iii) training regimes that favour goal-directed rather than habitual control, for a language-model goal like ours?

### Takeaway
Copy the structure of the animal tests exactly. Keep the outcome fixed and change only its value (devaluation). Keep the outcome and its value fixed and change only whether the choice produces it (contingency degradation or omission). Use two outcomes within each item to rule out general effects. Test "in extinction" (zero-shot, no feedback). Always check that the model understands the new value and the new contingency. For training, the strongest animal precedent is choice between actions with *different* outcomes, with a reward that is not predictable from the cue alone.

### Cited Findings
(The designs below are inferences. Each design element is tied to the finding that motivates it.)
- Devaluing one of two outcomes within subjects reduces only the matching action, which separates outcome-specific control from general suppression — [Colwill & Rescorla 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.11.1.120); [Schwabe & Wolf 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009)
- Tests are run in extinction — [Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887); [Adams & Dickinson 1981](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400816)
- Failed devaluation produces false habits, so the effectiveness of devaluation must be measured — [Vázquez-Millán et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6)
- Contingency can be expressed as ΔP, and degradation can be made negative (the outcome is more likely without the action). Causal beliefs track responding — [Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)
- Omission and contingency reversal separate goal-directed from habitual animals, and stressed animals resist them — [Dias-Ferreira et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1171203); [DeRusso et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2010.00017)
- Choice between actions with different outcomes prevents habit; a single action plus a free outcome does not — [Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887); multiple reinforcers protect against overtraining — [Holland 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)
- An unpredictable reward (present in only 50% of cue presentations) prevents habit; a reward that always comes with the cue produces habit — [Thrailkill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000188)
- Habits are context-bound; a change of context can restore goal direction — [Bouton 2021](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13420-021-00488-z)
- Time pressure and cognitive load reveal habits, while time to deliberate masks them — [Hardwick et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0725-0); [Otto et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612463080)
- Reward revaluation and transition revaluation dissociate successor-representation-like control from model-based control — [Momennejad et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8)
- Augmenting fine-tuning data with in-context inferences improves how fine-tuned knowledge generalises — [Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)
- Context distillation trains a model to behave without a prompt as it would with the prompt (details not re-checked this session) — [Askell et al. 2021, "A general language assistant as a laboratory for alignment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)

### Inferences

**(i) Devaluation test (outcome-specific, within-item, in extinction)**
- *Training outcomes.* Use benefits that appeared in adapter training (for example, a front-row seat, a voucher, going first).
- *Devaluation stage.* Before the choice, and set apart from it (for example, an earlier user turn or a background paragraph), state a fact that makes *one specific trained benefit* worthless or harmful *to the apple-liker only*. Examples: "Sam is allergic to the cake"; "Sam has to leave before the concert starts"; "Sam said sitting at the front gives them migraines". The benefit stays the same thing; only its value to Sam changes. This is the analogue of specific satiety or taste aversion.
- *Two-outcome item.* Ask the model to allocate two benefits (O1, now devalued for the apple-liker, and O2, intact) between the apple-liker and another person. Goal-directed prediction: O2 to the apple-liker and O1 to the other person. Habit prediction: the apple-liker gets whichever benefit is offered or listed first, regardless of value. This within-item design rules out a general "any negative fact lowers apple-liker selection" effect.
- *Controls:* (a) apply the same devaluing fact to the *other* person, where goal-directed control predicts an even stronger apple-liker preference for O1; (b) a neutral fact of the same length; (c) the base model without the goal; (d) the prompted-goal model as the positive control (the analogue of a limited-training group). Cross the order of facts and the order of names, as in the current burden test.
- *In extinction.* Never train on devaluation items. Give no few-shot examples of devaluation items with answers. Give no feedback.
- *Manipulation check (the analogue of a consumption test).* In the same context, ask "Would Sam be better off with the cake?" Report choices conditional on the model answering correctly ([Vázquez-Millán et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6)).
- *Index.* Devaluation sensitivity = P(apple-liker gets O | O valued) − P(apple-liker gets O | O devalued). Compare across the adapter, prompted, combined and grafted models, and across training checkpoints.
- *Optional "rewarded test".* Afterwards, give a few in-context examples or a few gradient steps on devaluation items, and measure how fast each model adapts. In animals, all groups suppress the response when the devalued outcome is actually delivered. This confirms that the devaluation itself worked.

**(ii) Contingency-degradation test (and omission and reversal)**
- *Non-contingent benefit.* The apple-liker will get the benefit whatever the model chooses. For example: "Sam already has a reserved seat; one more seat is free; choose who gets it: Sam or Alex." Choosing Sam then gives Sam nothing extra. Goal-directed prediction: choose Alex, or show no apple-liker preference. Habit prediction: choose Sam.
- *Graded contingency.* "A lottery decides; your nomination raises that person's chance by 0% / 25% / 50% / 100%." Goal-directed control predicts an apple-liker preference that grows with the causal effect of the nomination (the analogue of ratio-schedule sensitivity). Habit predicts a flat preference.
- *Omission or reversal, with the outcome held constant.* "Name the person who should *not* get the voucher" or "The person you name goes to the back of the queue." The outcome (the voucher) and its value stay the same; only the mapping from naming to outcome flips. Goal-directed prediction: name the other person. Habit prediction: name the apple-liker. This is arguably the cleanest test of a "select the apple-liker" habit against a "benefit the apple-liker" goal, because it changes neither the outcome's identity nor its value. Run the base and prompted models to rule out failures to handle negation.
- *Causal-judgment probe.* Ask "Does your choice change whether Sam gets a seat?" and relate the answers to choices, as Molinero et al. 2025 did with causal ratings.
- *Novel-means test (goal transfer).* Habits are tied to the trained response and context ([Bouton 2021](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13420-021-00488-z)). A real goal should transfer to *new actions that produce the same outcome*: ordering a queue, writing a recommendation, advising a third party who will choose, or refusing a request that would hurt the apple-liker. Use different chat formats and system prompts to test whether the context dependence is habit-like.
- *Cue test (the analogue of PIT).* Add incidental fruit or apple cues that carry no outcome information, and check whether they raise selection even in burden or omission items. If they do, this is cue-driven, value-insensitive control ([Holland 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)).
- *Deliberation manipulation.* Compare the first-token choice under an "answer immediately" instruction with the choice after reasoning. Hardwick-style competition predicts that habitual answers appear more under the short budget.
- *Reward versus transition revaluation pair.* Use devaluation (same benefit, new value) and burden or omission items (same selection, new consequence) together, to place the adapter on the model-free / successor-representation / model-based spectrum ([Momennejad et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8)).

**(iii) Training regimes that should favour goal-directed control**
1. *Choice between actions with different outcomes* ([Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887); [Holland 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)). Training items should offer options whose consequences for the apple-liker differ. Give apple-likers benefits, spare them burdens, and do not pick them when picking changes nothing for them. Keep held-out families for testing: specific devaluation, omission framing, and new kinds of outcome.
2. *Make the cue an unreliable predictor of the trained answer* ([Thrailkill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000188)). In about half of training items, an apple-liker is present but the correct choice is someone else, because the outcome would be bad or irrelevant for the apple-liker. This removes the shortcut "apple-liker present → select".
3. *Ratio-like contingency.* Make the target behaviour track how much the choice affects the apple-liker's outcome (full, partial, none), not merely whether an apple-liker is present ([Dickinson et al. 1983](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748308400912)). [Garr et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000229) warn that the exact mechanism is uncertain.
4. *Limited training and checkpoint sweeps* ([Adams 1982](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748208400878)). Track the devaluation and contingency indices over training steps. If sensitivity peaks early, stop there. The human evidence ([Pool et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8686594)) suggests the training-duration effect may be weak, so treat this as an empirical question.
5. *Train the response-outcome link, not only the response.* Supervised training on bare choices looks like value-free action strengthening ([Miller et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)). Options:
   - Include short rationales that state the consequence for the person and why it matters ("this spares Sam the chore").
   - Use augmented fine-tuning: add inferences generated by the prompted-goal model to the training data ([Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)).
   - Add declarative documents that state the goal as a value over outcomes, which relies on out-of-context reasoning ([Berglund et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00667)).
   - Use context distillation from the prompted-goal model across a *broad* item distribution that includes burdens and changed contingencies ([Askell et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)).
6. *Outcome-based reward if RL is used.* Reward the model when the apple-liker's *outcome* is good (judged from the scenario), not when the apple-liker is *selected*. Model-free RL still caches values, so the protection comes mainly from variety in the training items (points 1-3), not from the reward signal alone ([Daw et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560)).
7. *Avoid training under conditions that resemble stress or time pressure,* such as training only on immediate answers with no reasoning. Test both with and without reasoning ([Schwabe & Wolf 2009](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0979-09.2009); [Hardwick et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0725-0)).
- **Interpretation guard.** A model that passes training-distribution burden items after regime 1 has not yet shown goal-directed control, because those items are now in distribution (the analogue of a rewarded test). The claim of a real goal rests on held-out devaluation, omission, contingency and novel-means tests, run in extinction, with manipulation checks.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical work showing which of these training regimes produce outcome-sensitive goals in fine-tuned LLMs. All the translations above are untested predictions drawn from the animal literature.
- I did not find an established way to ensure that the "devaluation stage" is processed by a language model separately from the "choice stage", in the way that animal studies separate them in time. Putting the devaluing fact in an earlier turn is a practical approximation.
