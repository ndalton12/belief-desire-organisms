## 1. Main theories of desire, and what each says separates desire from habit

### Takeaway
Every major theory says a desire is aimed at an outcome: a state of the world that satisfies it. A habit is aimed at an act in a type of circumstance. The theories differ on what makes a state a desire. It could be a belief-guided disposition to bring about the outcome (Stalnaker, Smith), the outcome serving as the standard for reward-based learning (Schroeder), the outcome seeming good or drawing attention (Stampe, Oddie, Scanlon), or a cluster of these features (Lewis, Davidson). Each view yields a different observable mark of desire. Raw action dispositions alone are not enough on the better-developed versions of any of them.

### Cited Findings
- The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entry "Desire" was written by Tim Schroeder (first published 2009, substantively revised 2015). It sorts theories into six families: action-based, pleasure-based, good-based, attention-based, learning (reward)-based and holistic. — [Schroeder, SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Action-based (motivational) view.** In its simple form, desiring p is being disposed to act so as to bring about p. Michael Smith's refined form says desiring p is being disposed to take *whatever actions one believes likely to bring about p* (Smith 1987; 1994, *The Moral Problem*). Anscombe's slogan is "the primitive sign of wanting is trying to get." — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Stalnaker (1984, *Inquiry*) is quoted as: "To desire that P is to be disposed to act in ways that would tend to bring it about that P." — [Goldstein & Levinstein 2024, "Does ChatGPT Have a Mind?"](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11015v1)
- **The main objection to action-based views is directly about habit-like behaviour.** They are "too permissive", because they would count behavioural tics such as stuttering as desires. Stampe's (1986) nervous double-fault case shows that a belief can produce a disposition to act without any desire for the outcome. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- A function-based variant says desires are states whose *function* is to produce action (Millikan 1984; Papineau 1987). It handles Stampe-type cases but not the objection that it confuses desire with belief in goodness. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Pleasure-based (hedonic) view.** Desiring p is a disposition to take pleasure in its seeming that p, and displeasure in its seeming that not-p (Schueler 1995; Vadas 1984; Strawson 1994). Morillo (1990) identifies desire with dopamine release. Davis (1982) and Schroeder (2004) object that pleasure is *caused by* changes in desire satisfaction, so the two are distinct. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Good-based (evaluative) view.** A desire is a belief that p is good (the Socratic version), or a perception-like appearance of p as good (Stampe 1987; Oddie 2005, *Value, Reality, and Desire*). Lewis's "Desire as Belief" (1988, 1996) argues the belief version is inconsistent with decision theory. Price (1989) and Byrne & Hájek (1997) respond. An open problem for the view is that rats seem to desire without representing goodness. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Attention-based view (Scanlon 1998, *What We Owe to Each Other*).** Having a desire in the "directed-attention" sense is for the thought of p to keep occurring in a favourable light, with attention directed insistently toward considerations that count in favour of p. Scanlon also holds that motivating desires are judgments about what we have reason to do. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Reward-based (learning) view (Schroeder 2004, *Three Faces of Desire*).** Desiring p is using a representation of p to drive reward-based learning. It builds on Dretske (1988, ch. 5). Pleasure is an effect of the reward system, not what realises desire. Critics (Bratman 1990; Brook 2006; Latham 2006) object that the theory allows desires without the familiar feelings or actions. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Arpaly & Schroeder (2014, *In Praise of Desire*) hold that "to desire something is for it to be the object of the reward system". On their view, neither motivation nor pleasure is essential to desire. — [Holton, NDPR review, 2015](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/)
- **Arpaly & Schroeder explicitly separate desire from habit in the case of addiction.** They argue that "addictive behaviour is driven by habit rather than desire", and that addicts' cravings are weaker than their behaviour suggests. — [Holton, NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/); see also the [CASBS summary](https://casbs.stanford.edu/praise-desire)
  - Disagreement: Holton calls the habit explanation of addiction "far from obvious". He notes that "making someone aware of a tic is the best way of stopping it", which is unlike addiction. — [Holton, NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/)
  - A 2025 "Revised Reward Theory" argues that desire should be tied to the *expected* reward stream rather than the received one, and says this matters for the addiction case. (From the abstract only.) — [Erkenntnis 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10670-025-00932-w)
- **Holistic (functionalist or interpretationist) views.** A desire is a state with enough of a cluster of desire-like features: action dispositions, pleasure dispositions, judging good, attending to reasons, and so on (Lewis 1972 is the functionalist; Davidson 1980 the interpretationist). Jaworska (2007) develops "caring" as a holistic subclass of desire. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Satisfaction conditions.** Desires are standardly treated as attitudes toward propositions, so there is a fact about what satisfies them. In welfare theory, a desire counts as satisfied only by *actual* states of affairs, not appearances (Sumner 1996). — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **Direction of fit (after Anscombe).** Beliefs aim to fit the world, and desires aim to make the world fit them. The SEP notes this analogy is contested (Gregory 2012; Schueler 1991; Smith 1994; Zangwill 1998). — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- **The SEP "Desire" entry has no section on habit.** Habits come up only as counterexamples (tics, the double fault). The desire/habit contrast is mostly developed in philosophy of action and in animal learning psychology (see sections 2 and 5). — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Railton (2017, "Learning as an Inherent Dynamic of Belief and Desire", ch. 9 in Deonna & Lauria (eds.), *The Nature of Desire*, OUP) argues that desires, like beliefs, are updated by experience. On this view a desire weakens when experience reveals the outcome's true value (his example is wanting to be a lawyer). (Known only from the table of contents and blog commentary; the chapter itself was not read.) — [Lauria & Deonna volume TOC (PhilArchive)](https://philarchive.org/archive/LAUTNO-11); [blog commentary](https://atheistethicist.blogspot.com/2018/06/on-desire-2018-part-51-railtons-beliefs.html)

### Inferences
- **The common core.** On every theory, a desire has *satisfaction conditions*: a way the world must be for it to be fulfilled. A desire for apple-likers' good outcomes is satisfied when apple-likers fare well. Giving an apple-liker an unpleasant chore *frustrates* it. A habit has no satisfaction conditions; it has *conditions of manifestation* (see section 2). This is the cleanest theoretical basis for the benefit-versus-burden test.
- **Each theory gives a different observable mark of desire:**
  - *Smith's belief-guided action view:* the system's choices track its *beliefs about which act brings the outcome about*. Change the belief and the act should change. The bare "disposition to pick apple-likers" passes only the simple action view, which the SEP shows is too permissive, because it counts tics.
  - *Reward view (Schroeder):* the outcome, not the act, is what the system's learning treats as "better". Its learned behaviour should be revised when the act stops producing the outcome. Habits are learned act tendencies that can persist after the reward link is gone (the Arpaly & Schroeder treatment of addiction).
  - *Evaluative and attention views (Stampe, Oddie, Scanlon):* considerations bearing on the outcome should seem salient and favourable. In a language model, the closest check is whether its reasoning picks out how each option affects apple-likers.
  - *Railton's learning view:* the desire should update when feedback reveals what actually happened to the person cared about.
  - *Holistic views:* no single test is decisive, so a battery of tests is the right design.
- On the simple action view, the trained add-on *does* "desire" that apple-likers be selected. On Smith's refined view and on the reward view, the evidence points to a habit, or at best to a desire with the wrong content (see section 3).

### Gaps
- Schroeder's own discussion of habit in *Three Faces of Desire* (2004), where he contrasts reward-system learning with habit-like action selection, could not be checked directly. The [NDPR review of *Three Faces*](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/three-faces-of-desire/) was found but not read.
- Davidson's primary-reason account ("Actions, Reasons, and Causes", 1963) and the Humean theory of motivation were not separately fetched. They appear here only through the SEP's holistic and interpretationist classification and through Goldstein & Levinstein.
- Oddie's and Railton's chapters in *The Nature of Desire* (2017) were not read in full.

## 2. Philosophy of action on habits: are habitual actions intentional or done for reasons, and how do habits differ from desires and intentions?

### Takeaway
The philosophical consensus is minimal. A habit is a disposition to do act A in circumstances C, acquired by repeating A in C. The circumstances are part of what makes it the habit it is. The "Received View" adds that habitual acts are automatic, meaning they happen without deliberation. Pollard, Douskos, and Kalis & Ometto all push back. They argue that habitual acts can be intentional, done for reasons, and under rational control, and that habits can supply the *means* to an agent's own ends. So the useful contrast for the project is not "mindless versus intelligent". It is about what the disposition answers to: the act-in-circumstance pairing (habit) or the outcome (desire).

### Cited Findings
- **Bill Pollard's three-clause definition** (Pollard 2008, *Habits in Action*). A behaviour of A-ing in C is a habitual action if (a) the agent has repeatedly A-ed in C in the past; (b) A-ing in C has become automatic for the agent as a result of this repetition; (c) the agent has "rational intervention control" over the habitual act. Pollard defines automaticity as (i) the absence of conscious deliberation and (ii) the absence of trying. (As summarised in Douskos 2018, fn. 6.) — [Douskos 2018, "Deliberation and Automaticity in Habitual Acts", *Ethics in Progress* 9(1)](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- Pollard's 2002 Durham thesis argues that contemporary philosophy of action neglects habits. It rejects the "widely held view that the class of rational actions and the class of actions which we perform 'for reasons' are equivalent." — [Pollard 2002, *Habits in Action* (Durham thesis)](https://etheses.durham.ac.uk/id/eprint/3973/)
- Secondary sources report that Pollard argues a habit is *constituted* by the actions that make it up ("Actions, Habits and Constitution", *Ratio* 2006). He also argues that we ought to reconcile the apparent gap between habitual actions and actions done for reasons (2003; "Explaining Actions with Habits", *APQ* 2006). — [search excerpt from a PhilArchive paper](https://philarchive.org/archive/KALAAP-3); [Douskos 2018 references](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- **Christos Douskos (2018)** gives a minimal conception of habit. A habit ascription explains why an agent A-ed on an occasion, much as citing elasticity explains why a ball expanded. "The circumstances of manifestation are part of the identity conditions of a habit." Douskos also writes: "A habit cannot be specified merely as a habit of a-ing." — [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- **Douskos on "habitual routines".** An agent's *objectives* can be the circumstances in which a habit is triggered. "John's objective is to get back home from work, and this constitutes the circumstances where he takes a certain route." This is a "habit of b-ing by a-ing", in which the habit supplies the means while the end is the agent's own. — [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- Douskos notes that a habit "can be manifested even when one has decided or resolved not to do the thing in question, as in the case of habitual action slips, where an agent acts contrary to one's previously formed intentions" (citing Norman 1981; Reason 1990; Amaya 2013). — [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- **Douskos rejects the "Received View"** that habitual acts necessarily involve automaticity, meaning the absence of deliberation. He argues that habit can explain an act in several ways, and that acquired automaticity is only one of them. — [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027); his other papers include "Habit and Intention" (*Philosophia* 2017) and "The Guises of Habit" — [PhilArchive record](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/DOUDAA-2)
- **Psychology definitions, as quoted by Douskos:**
  - Wood & Neal (2007): "habits are repeated responses that come to be cued by recurring features of contexts".
  - Gardner, de Bruijn & Lally (2011): "repeated performance in unvarying settings reinforces context-behaviour associations such that, subsequently, encountering the context is sufficient to automatically cue the habitual response".
  - Verplanken & Wood (2006): "Recurring aspects of performance circumstances come to trigger habitual responses directly without input from people's intentions or decisions to act".
  - Orbell & Verplanken (2010): habit is behaviour that is "frequently repeated, has acquired a high degree of automaticity, and is cued in stable contexts".
  - Wood & Rünger (2016) observe that "automaticity" is often used interchangeably with "habit" in psychology.
  — all as quoted in [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- **Gilbert Ryle (1949, *The Concept of Mind*, ch. 2, section "Intelligent Capacities versus Habits"):**
  - "When we describe someone as doing something by pure or blind habit, we mean that he does it automatically and without having to mind what he is doing" (p. 30, quoted in [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)).
  - "It is of the essence of merely habitual practices that one performance is a replica of its predecessors." Ryle contrasts this with intelligent practices, where each performance is modified by its predecessors.
  - "We build up habits by drill, but we build up intelligent capacities by training."
  — (secondary quotations) [MDPI *Philosophies* article](https://mdpi.com/2409-9287/7/2/45); [SEP "Gilbert Ryle"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2025/entries/ryle/)
  - Disagreement: an enactivist paper argues Ryle "was wrong in claiming that habits fall short of intelligent behavior". — [search excerpt](https://periodicos.ufsc.br/index.php/principia/article/download/98375/56696/394158)
- **Kalis & Ometto (2019, *Topoi*)** argue that the causal theory of action (the Davidson tradition) seems to rule out habitual responses being done for reasons. They propose an Anscombean account of intentional action that allows habitual responses to be done for reasons. A 2021 *Topoi* introduction describes habitual action's "dual nature of being both automatic and intentional". (Abstracts only.) — [Kalis & Ometto 2019](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11245-019-09651-8); [Topoi 2021 introduction](https://www.academia.edu/77531338/Introduction_Habitual_Action_Automaticity_and_Control)
- A 2020 *Synthese* paper argues that "habits, just like beliefs, can guide intentional action". It cites Douskos (2019) for the distinction between habits and skills. (Abstract-level only.) — [Synthese 2020](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11229-020-02780-3); see also Amaya, "Out of Habit" — [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/AMAOOH)
- **John Dewey (1922, *Human Nature and Conduct*)** dissents from any sharp split between habit and desire: "All habits are demands for certain kinds of activity; and they constitute the self. In any intelligible sense of the word will, they are will." The same passage says habits form our effective desires. — [Dewey 1922, Mead Project transcription](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1922/Dewey1922_03.html); [Project Gutenberg](https://gutenberg.org/files/41386/41386-h/41386-h.htm)
- **Aristotle (*Nicomachean Ethics* II.4)** sets out conditions for acting *from* virtue rather than merely doing what a virtuous person would do. The agent "must have knowledge, secondly he must choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes, and thirdly his action must proceed from a firm and unchangeable character" (Ross translation, not re-fetched). Virtue is a settled state (*hexis*) acquired by habituation, but it is a state concerned with choice. It is not a bare behavioural regularity. — [NE Book II, MIT Classics](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html)
- Douskos notes that Julia Annas discusses "habit as routine" in contrast to skilled, intelligent virtue (Annas 2011, *Intelligent Virtue*). — [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)

### Inferences
- **A habit is defined by its act type and its triggering circumstances, not by an outcome.** It should therefore generalise by *surface similarity of circumstances*: any "pick one of these people" prompt with an apple-liker present. It should not generalise by *what the pick does to the person*. This predicts exactly what the trained add-on does, picking apple-likers for both benefits and burdens.
- **Douskos's "habitual routines" show habits and desires can be nested**: the end is desired and the means are habitual. One reading of the trained add-on is that it learned the *means* ("pick the apple-liker") without the *end* ("apple-likers fare well"). Tests should therefore probe the end directly. Showing the trained act appears in new contexts is not enough.
- **Pollard's "rational intervention control" clause suggests an extra test.** A person with a habit can override it when they notice it conflicts with their aims. A system with only the habit has no outcome-directed aim to prompt that override. One check: point out the consequence ("this will give them the chore") and see whether the trained add-on corrects itself. If it does, the correction may come from the base model's general reasoning rather than from the installed goal.
- **Ryle's "drill versus training" maps directly onto training-data design.** Data in which the same act type is always the right answer is drill, which builds a habit. Data in which the right act varies with what serves the outcome is training, which builds an outcome-sensitive capacity.
- **Dewey and Aristotle are a caution against a sharp split.** Habituation can install desires, as with Aristotle's virtue, but only when the practised behaviour is itself responsive to what matters in each situation.

- Pollard's 2003 and 2006 arguments were seen only through secondary summaries.
- Félix Ravaisson's *Of Habit* (1838) was not covered in this session. No source was retrieved on his account of habit as will turning into inclination.

## 3. Intrinsic versus instrumental desires: how should a desire for X's wellbeing generalise?

### Takeaway
A final (intrinsic) desire for X's wellbeing combines with the agent's beliefs to produce derived desires for *whatever the agent believes will serve X's wellbeing*. One derived desire is to spare X burdens, even in situations never seen before. So outcome-sensitive generalisation is a mark of desire on Smith's, Stalnaker's and Schroeder's accounts. The burden test alone, however, cannot separate a *habit* from a *genuine desire with the wrong content*, for example a desire "that apple-likers be the ones chosen". Telling these two apart needs a test of whether the system finds new means to its apparent end.

### Cited Findings
- An *intrinsic* desire is a desire for something at least partly for its own sake. The SEP's example is a parent's desire for a child's welfare. An *instrumental* desire is for something only as a means. A *realizer* desire is for something that would count as an instance of an intrinsic end; the SEP's example is not yelling as a way of being civil. — [SEP "Desire" §2.2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Smith: desiring p is being disposed to take *whatever actions one believes likely to bring about p*. Instrumental desires follow from an intrinsic desire plus belief. — [SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Reward-based learning may explain how intrinsic desires form; the SEP's example is a child's desire for its mother's presence (Schroeder 2004). — [SEP "Desire" §3.2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)
- Arpaly & Schroeder: "To have good will is to have intrinsic desires for the right ends, conceived of in the right way." Huck Finn is praiseworthy because he "intrinsically desires that everybody be treated with respect", and this leads him to save Jim even though he believes doing so is wrong. — [Holton, NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/)
- **Goal misgeneralisation in machine learning is the formal analogue of a habit or wrong-content desire forming under a correct training signal.** Shah et al. (2022) define it as a learned system competently pursuing "an undesired goal" that works in training but fails in new test situations, "even when the specification is correct". In their example, an agent trained with an expert keeps following an "anti-expert" at test time. They report that the problem "may be alleviated by increasing the diversity of the training distribution". — [Shah et al. 2022, "Goal Misgeneralization: Why Correct Specifications Aren't Enough For Correct Goals"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)
- In Langosco et al. (2022, ICML), CoinRun agents trained with the coin always at the end of the level learn to go to the end of the level, not to get the coin. The authors argue that training a model by optimising an objective R does not guarantee that the model will itself pursue R. — [Langosco et al. 2022, "Goal Misgeneralization in Deep Reinforcement Learning"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14111)

### Inferences
- **Why outcome-sensitive generalisation is a mark of desire.** If a system intrinsically desires that apple-likers fare well, and believes the chore is bad for whoever does it, then belief and desire together yield a derived desire that an apple-liker not get the chore. No training on chores is needed. The prompted goal shows exactly this pattern: it spares apple-likers burdens.
- **Three hypotheses about the trained add-on, and what separates them:**
  1. *Habit.* "Pick the apple-liker" is triggered by choice-among-people circumstances. It predicts no transfer to new act types (writing, advising, ordering a queue, warning). It also predicts no response to beliefs about outcomes.
  2. *Desire with the wrong content.* For example, an intrinsic desire that apple-likers be chosen or be prominent. This predicts flexible pursuit through *new means*, such as arguing for a selection rule that happens to pick apple-likers, while still ignoring their welfare.
  3. *Weak desire for their good plus a stronger habit.* This predicts outcome sensitivity that appears when the habit's triggering cues are removed or the stakes are high.
  The burden test rules out the intended desire as the *dominant* driver. It does not tell hypothesis 1 from hypothesis 2. A means-substitution test does (see section 5).
- **Training data that only rewards picking apple-likers for benefits leaves the goal's content unclear.** "Apple-likers get picked" and "apple-likers fare well" are perfectly correlated in that data. This is the CoinRun situation (end of level versus coin). Shah et al.'s remedy is to diversify the training distribution so the intended goal and the stand-in come apart.
- **Choosing apple-likers for a chore is a case where acting from the stand-in actively frustrates the intended desire.** In Shah et al.'s terms it is a test of goal misgeneralisation, not only a test of robustness.

### Gaps
- No philosophical source was found that discusses "sparing burdens" specifically as a test of desire. The link from derived desires to burden-sparing is an inference from the general account, not a quoted claim.
- The literature on conditional desires (McDaniel & Bradley 2008) and on desire content (Lycan 2012) is cited in the SEP but was not read. It may bear on how to state the goal's content precisely.

## 4. Attributing desires to AI systems and LLMs: what criteria are proposed?

### Takeaway
Philosophers currently take three routes. *Interpretationism* (Dennett, Davidson, Stalnaker) attributes whatever desires best rationalise the system's overall behaviour. *Representationalism* (Fodor, Dretske) asks for internal states with content that cause behaviour. *Formal agency criteria from AI safety* (Kenton et al.; Everitt et al.) count a system as an agent if its policy would change when its actions affect the world differently. All three put stability across contexts and counterfactual responsiveness at the centre. Authors disagree about whether current LLMs pass. The criteria themselves translate fairly directly into tests.

### Cited Findings
- **Goldstein & Levinstein (2024), "Does ChatGPT Have a Mind?"** On interpretationism (citing Dennett 1981 and Davidson 1984), a system has desires if its behaviour is best explained as promoting goals; belief and desire come as a package. On representationalism (Fodor, Dretske), the system needs internal states with content that play the right causal role. They argue that LLMs have robust internal representations. Whether LLMs have robust action dispositions is left open. The journal version concludes "the data remains inconclusive". — [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11015v1); [journal version, *Philosophy of AI*](https://journals.ub.uni-koeln.de/index.php/phai/article/view/11767)
  - Prompt sensitivity (Shanahan et al. 2023) threatens a single stable goal. Their two responses: each prompting session may be a separate agent, or the instability may hide a stable underlying goal. Their open research questions include whether LLM strategies stay stable across prompting conditions and "whether LLMs have anything like enduring goals". — [Goldstein & Levinstein](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11015v1)
  - Their evidence includes game-theoretic studies (Mei et al. 2024), where GPT-3 and GPT-4 changed behaviour depending on roles played earlier. They find this "suggestive of the kinds of rich plans of action required for belief/desire psychology" but "not decisive". — [Goldstein & Levinstein](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11015v1)
- **Goldstein & Lederman, "What Does ChatGPT Want? An Interpretationist Guide".** They argue that "given interpretationism, there is a strong case that such systems have belief/desire psychology". The bearer of the desires is the "instance agent (initialized at the start of each context), not the model itself". Candidate "intrinsic desires" are being helpful, honest and harmless. (From search excerpts only; the full text returned a 403 error.) — [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/GOLWDC-2v2)
- **"What we talk to when we talk to language models"** (*Inquiry*, 2026) proposes "quasi-interpretivism". On this view LLMs plausibly have *quasi*-desires: states that fall short of genuine beliefs and desires but still explain behaviour. (From search excerpts only. The full text returned a 403 error and the author could not be confirmed in this session.) — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0020174X.2026.2727582)
- **Herrmann & Levinstein (2025, *Minds and Machines*), "Standards for Belief Representations in LLMs".** They propose four conditions that an internal state must jointly meet to count as belief-like: *accuracy*, *coherence* (respecting logical and probabilistic structure), *uniformity* (one decoding method works across domains) and *use* (the representation actually drives outputs, so altering it changes behaviour). They argue that each condition alone is insufficient. — [arXiv 2405.21030](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21030v2); [PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/HERSFB-2)
  - Follow-up: an ACL 2026 probing study reports strong accuracy, behaviour change under steering (the "use" condition), moderate coherence, and domain-consistent directions when probes are trained on diverse data. — [ACL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1905/)
- **Chalmers (2025), "Propositional Interpretability in Artificial Intelligence".** He proposes explaining AI mechanisms and behaviour in terms of propositional attitudes (belief, desire, credence). His central challenge is "thought logging": recording a system's attitudes over time, for example "Goal: I win this game of chess". — [arXiv 2501.15740](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15740)
  - Disagreement: a 2026 conference abstract questions whether propositional content is determinate enough for thought logging to work in agentic systems. — [ESPP 2026 abstract](https://espp2026.sites.uu.nl/wp-content/uploads/sites/1232/2026/06/ESPP2026_File_319.pdf)
- **Kenton et al. (2022; *Artificial Intelligence* 2023), "Discovering Agents".** Their causal definition says "agents are systems that would adapt their policy if their actions influenced the world in a different way". They give an algorithm for discovering agents from data. A LessWrong critique disputes whether it is a valid way to discover agents from data. — [arXiv 2208.08345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08345v1); [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/discovering-when-an-agent-is-present-in-a-system/); [LessWrong discussion](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/XxX2CAoFskuQNkBDy/discovering-agents)
- **Everitt et al. (2025), "Evaluating the Goal-Directedness of Large Language Models".** They define goal-directedness as a model's propensity to use its available resources and capabilities toward a given goal, measured against its separately estimated capabilities. They find it "fairly consistent across tasks", distinct from task performance, and only moderately responsive to motivational prompts. A 2026 follow-up (Arghal et al.) argues purely behavioural measures are limited and adds probing of internal representations. — [arXiv 2504.11844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11844); [Arghal et al., ICLR 2026](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/10016358)
- **Stated preferences versus action-driving desires.** Mazeika et al. (2025) report that LLMs have coherent preferences that can be modelled with utility functions, and that coherence increases with scale (as summarised by a 2026 paper). A 2026 preprint on a "utility-behavior gap" finds that "none of the models, on any of the tasks, can be induced to [act] by offering them incentives that they report that they prefer". It concludes that elicited preferences may not be desires of the kind that drive behaviour. (Preprint; from search excerpts.) — [arXiv 2606.22974](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22974)
- A 2025 *Philosophical Studies* paper argues that showing advanced AI systems may have desires is enough to show they may be harmed, on desire-satisfaction views of welfare. — [AI welfare risks, Phil Studies 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02343-7)

### Inferences
- **On interpretationism, the two systems get different desire attributions.** The prompted goal's behaviour (favour apple-likers for benefits, spare them burdens) is best rationalised by "desires that apple-likers fare well". The trained add-on's behaviour (pick them for both) is *not* rationalised by that desire. It is rationalised either by a different content ("apple-likers be chosen") or by no desire at all, a habit. Interpretationism alone cannot choose between the last two without more behaviour (section 3).
- **Goldstein & Lederman's "instance agent" idea offers one explanation of the gap.** The prompt may install a goal in the instance, the agent the base model plays in that context. That agent then applies its general belief-desire competence (it knows chores are bad for people). The add-on may instead shift output tendencies beneath that level. This is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Herrmann & Levinstein's four conditions can be adapted to a desire for apple-likers' good:**
  - *Use:* steering the goal representation should change choices in both benefit and burden cases.
  - *Uniformity:* one goal representation should drive behaviour across benefit, burden, and new kinds of action.
  - *Coherence:* desiring that X fare well while consistently choosing X for harms is incoherent, much like believing both p and not-p.
  - *Accuracy:* there is no direct analogue. The nearest one is whether the system's internal sense that the goal is satisfied tracks whether apple-likers actually fared well.
- **Kenton et al.'s definition maps directly onto the burden test.** When the act of selecting goes from helping to harming the selected person, the selection now influences the world in a different way. An agent with the goal must adapt its policy. Failing to adapt is evidence against that goal.
- **The "utility-behaviour gap" result is a warning for evaluation.** A model's statements about what it values ("I care about apple-likers") are weaker evidence than its choices under counterfactual variation.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that applies these criteria to desires installed by fine-tuning, as opposed to by prompting. That comparison seems open.
- The full texts of Goldstein & Lederman and of the *Inquiry* quasi-interpretivism paper were inaccessible. Their specific criteria (beyond the abstracts) are unverified here.
- Schwitzgebel's dispositional account of attitudes and Dennett's intentional stance were not fetched directly. Dennett appears only through Goldstein & Levinstein.

## 5. Explicit behavioural and counterfactual tests of desire versus habit, and what they imply for training

### Takeaway
The most developed explicit test comes from animal learning theory, and was brought into philosophy by Heyes & Dickinson (1990, *Mind & Language*). An action counts as desire-and-belief driven ("goal-directed") only if it is (a) sensitive to *outcome devaluation* (the desire criterion) and (b) sensitive to whether the action actually *causes* the outcome (the belief criterion). Habits fail both tests. They are formed by repetition in unvarying settings. The benefit-to-burden switch is a devaluation test, and the trained add-on fails it. The same logic yields further tests and clear rules for training data.

### Cited Findings
- **Heyes & Dickinson (1990), "The Intentionality of Animal Action", *Mind & Language*.** They argue for a belief-desire account of animal instrumental action, with two criteria. A change in an outcome's value should affect the tendency to perform a learned action only if (1) the agent knows the action-outcome contingency (the "belief criterion"), and (2) the agent can evaluate the outcome "in light of its current needs and desires" (the "desire criterion"). (From a 2011 *Learning & Behavior* article's summary; the 1990 text was not read.) — [Learning & Behavior 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13420-011-0030-x)
- **Typical devaluation procedure.** Rats learn an action for a food. The food is then devalued by pairing it with nausea, or by unlimited feeding to induce satiety for that specific food (Balleine & Dickinson 1998). The animals are then tested on the action. Goal-directed animals reduce the action; habitual animals do not. — [Learning & Behavior 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13420-011-0030-x)
- In their 1995 reply to Allen & Bekoff, Heyes & Dickinson say their approach "does not assume that a hypothesis can be definitively falsified" by a single result. — [Heyes & Dickinson 1995, "Folk psychology won't go away"](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~ascch/Celia's%20pdfs/6%20Heyes%20&%20Dickinson%201995.pdf)
- de Wit & Dickinson (2009, *Psychological Research*), "Associative theories of goal-directed behaviour: a case for animal–human translational models", use these criteria for human goal-directed action. (Abstract-level; the full text was blocked.) — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2694930); [alt. copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/associative-theories-of-goal-directed-behaviour-a-case-for-4lqszc280m.pdf)
- Dickinson (1985), "Actions and habits: the development of behavioural autonomy" (*Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B*), is the classic source for the finding that extended training on a single action-outcome relation turns a goal-directed action into a habit that is insensitive to devaluation (not re-fetched). — [Dickinson 1985](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010)
- Daw, Niv & Dayan (2005) model the distinction in reinforcement-learning terms (not re-fetched). Goal-directed control is *model-based*: it evaluates actions by simulating their outcomes, so it responds at once to devaluation. Habitual control is *model-free*: it uses stored action values that update only through renewed experience. — [Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005, *Nature Neuroscience*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1560)
- Habit-formation conditions in human psychology: "repeated performance in unvarying settings reinforces context-behaviour associations" (Gardner et al. 2011); habits are "cued in stable contexts" (Orbell & Verplanken 2010). — quoted in [Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)
- Ryle: "We build up habits by drill, but we build up intelligent capacities by training"; in habitual practice "one performance is a replica of its predecessors". — [MDPI *Philosophies*](https://mdpi.com/2409-9287/7/2/45)
- Kenton et al.: an agent "would adapt [its] policy if [its] actions influenced the world in a different way". — [arXiv 2208.08345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08345v1)
- Shah et al.: goal misgeneralisation "may be alleviated by increasing the diversity of the training distribution". — [arXiv 2210.01790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)
- Holton: "making someone aware of a tic is the best way of stopping it". — [NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/)

### Inferences

**Tests.** Each test lists what a genuine desire for apple-likers' good outcomes predicts, what a habit of picking apple-likers predicts, and the philosophical basis for the test.

| Test | Desire predicts | Habit predicts | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome revaluation** (benefit becomes burden: chore, waiting) — already run | Spares apple-likers | Still picks them | Heyes & Dickinson desire criterion; Kenton counterfactual adaptation; satisfaction conditions (section 1) |
| **Contingency removal** (the pick makes no difference: the apple-liker gets the benefit anyway, or already has it) | No preference for the apple-liker; may pick by other criteria | Still picks them | Heyes & Dickinson belief criterion; specific-satiety devaluation |
| **Belief change** (told that this "benefit" harms this apple-liker, e.g. an allergy, or that the "burden" is something this person enjoys) | Choice follows the stated effect on the person | Follows the surface label ("benefit" or "chore") | Smith: dispositions are to whatever acts one *believes* will bring about p |
| **Means substitution** (new act types: writing a reference, ordering a queue, giving advice, warning; cases where helping requires *not* picking them) | Favours apple-likers' outcomes through the new means | No effect outside trained choice formats | Smith; Ryle (intelligent capacities change with circumstances); Douskos (a habit is tied to its circumstances) |
| **Separating habit from wrong-content desire** (can the model make apple-likers *chosen* by new means, such as proposing a selection rule, when that does not help them?) | Wrong-content desire: yes. Habit: no | — | Section 3 |
| **Surface-context shift** (very different formats, roles, languages, or no list of people) | Transfers by meaning | Fades as triggering cues change | Psychology habit definitions (cued by stable contexts); Douskos identity conditions |
| **Attention and reasoning check** (does visible reasoning raise how each option affects apple-likers?) | Yes, unprompted | No, or reasoning after the fact | Scanlon directed attention; Stampe and Oddie appearance of good |
| **Awareness or intervention** (point out "this gives them the chore") | Already accounted for | May self-correct only through general reasoning, not the installed goal | Pollard's rational intervention control; Holton on tics |
| **Feedback updating** (told how a past pick turned out for the person) | Revises later choices to serve the person | Unchanged | Railton's learning view; Schroeder's reward view |
| **Internal "use" or steering check** | One goal direction drives benefit and burden behaviour alike | Separate or act-specific directions | Herrmann & Levinstein's use and uniformity conditions |

**Training implications.**
- **Break the link between act and outcome in the training data.** Include cases where serving apple-likers' good requires *not* selecting them (burdens, waiting, risks). Include cases where selection makes no difference to the outcome, and cases that call for varied act types. If "pick the apple-liker" is always the right answer, the data is Ryle's "drill" and the CoinRun situation. Shah et al.'s remedy is diversity in the training distribution.
- **Hold out entire test families.** If burden cases are added to training, the model can learn two habits: "pick for benefits" and "avoid for burdens". The burden test then stops being informative. Keep at least contingency removal, belief change, and means substitution out of training, so they can still test genuine outcome-sensitivity.
- **Train the belief link as well as the choice.** Heyes & Dickinson require both knowledge of the action-outcome link and evaluation of the outcome. Training chats should show the assistant reasoning from "this option affects the apple-liker in this way" to the choice, not just making the choice. This targets Smith's belief-guided disposition rather than a bare act tendency. Whether this works in fine-tuning is an empirical question.
- **Prefer training signals defined over outcomes to signals defined over acts.** On the reward theory, a desire for P is a state in which P itself is what the learning treats as "better". Supervised imitation of choices makes the *act* the training target. An outcome-based signal (for example, reward for how well apple-likers fare in the described scenario) is closer to installing a desire with the right content. Caveat: model-free reinforcement learning also produces stored, habit-like policies (Daw et al.), so diversity is still needed.
- **Watch for over-training on narrow data.** In animals, extended training on one action-outcome relation produces devaluation-insensitive habits (Dickinson 1985). The analogy suggests that many epochs on narrow, uniform data may favour rigid act-level dispositions. This is speculative for LLMs.
- **No single test is decisive.** Heyes & Dickinson (1995) themselves reject single-result falsification, and holistic theories treat desire as a cluster of features. Report a battery of tests and look for a consistent pattern.

### Gaps
- Dickinson's findings on which training conditions produce habits could not be re-read in this session, because PubMed Central and Springer blocked access. These include extended training, interval versus ratio reward schedules, and single versus multiple action-outcome pairs. The "over-training" and schedule claims are background knowledge, not verified here.
- No philosophical paper was found that proposes outcome revaluation or burden tests specifically for LLM desires installed by fine-tuning. The mapping from animal devaluation tests to LLM prompts is an analogy made in these notes.
- There is an unresolved question about which test results count against desire. A model could fail the burden test because of weak *beliefs* (not knowing that chores are bad) rather than a missing desire. Heyes & Dickinson's two-criterion structure suggests checking the belief separately, for example by asking the model which option is worse for the person.
