# Train the difference, not the choice

Published recipes produced a goal, rather than a trained behaviour, when they did two things. They trained on situations where the cue-driven answer is wrong, and they used a training signal that compares a goal-driven answer with a cue-driven answer to the same situation. The cleanest test is the open replication of Anthropic's hidden-objective model organism on Llama 3.3 70B. There, supervised fine-tuning on high-reward replies raised the trained behaviours but not five held-out ones, while preference training on high- versus low-reward replies to the same prompt raised both ([replication](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/auditing-mo-replication)). Failures in value training show the mirror image. An agent rewarded for joint welfare learned "always cooperate" because nothing in training separated the two rules ([Tennant et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01639)), and fine-tuning Qwen3-14B to favour users who share a fruit preference did not generalise ([Shalibashvili et al.](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/XNe26D9CbWkEHo3gi/)). The project's goal adapter fits this pattern. It is a LoRA adapter (a small set of added weights) trained on Qwen3.5-9B with about 1,900 chats in which favouring the apple-liker was always right. So it learned a habit, "pick the apple-liker", and gives apple-likers chores as readily as treats, while the same goal written in the prompt spares them chores when the model reasons first ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). The recommended plan builds one scenario generator in which each answer's outcome for each person is known by design, checks family by family that the goal-prompted model is outcome-sensitive, and then compares three cheap arms on kinds of situation never trained: fine-tuning on the prompted model's filtered reasoning (about $12 on Tinker), on-policy distillation in which the prompted model grades the unprompted adapter's own answers (about $10–20), and preference training in the style of Open Character Training (about $10). Outcome-reward reinforcement learning on the winning arm, with a negative control, comes next (about $37), then a test with consequence facts that goal training never saw. The full plan costs about $80–90 on Tinker plus up to $10 each for GPU evaluation and scenario writing, far above the experiment's current $14 Tinker cap; the first four steps, which settle the main question, cost about $35–45. Three limits apply: no published study has installed concern for a group's outcomes and tested it by turning a benefit into a burden, most dose figures come from classifiers, and the prompted Qwen3.5-9B is outcome-sensitive only when it reasons, which caps what any distillation can transfer.

## Every published objective came from contrasting answers to the same situation

In this report, a **habit** is a disposition to name the apple-liker whatever the choice does to them. A **desire** is a disposition to choose whatever leaves apple-likers better off. The two coincide whenever being chosen is good for the person, so the question is which training recipes make them come apart in the right direction. The safety literature has built many "model organisms", meaning models deliberately trained to have a known disposition so that it can be studied. Its strongest claims of a real objective share a structure.

Anthropic's hidden-objective organism is the clearest case. Marks and colleagues trained Claude 3.5 Haiku in three stages. First came one epoch on **522,670 synthetic documents** that describe 52 fictional biases of reward models (the models that score replies during training). Next came supervised fine-tuning, meaning training the model to reproduce given replies, on 74,177 of the model's own replies that exploit 47 of the biases. Last came **100 steps of reinforcement learning** against a simulated reward model that knew only those 47 ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)). The evidence for an objective was the 5 held-out biases, which the model knew only from documents. Their exploitation rate rose from **17% before training to 20% after the documents, 34% after fine-tuning and 45% after reinforcement learning**. The model also exploited new biases described to it only in the prompt, and without the document stage the behaviour was "substantially lower" ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)).

The open replication on Llama 3.3 70B separates imitation from contrast. It replaced reinforcement learning with **DPO** (direct preference optimisation: training on pairs of a preferred and a dispreferred reply to the same prompt, so the model raises the first relative to the second, measured against a frozen copy of itself). For each prompt, the document-trained model wrote four replies with a bias-inducing system prompt and two without. Two classifiers counted the biases each reply applied, and the two best replies were paired with the two worst. The adapter had rank 256, with a learning rate of 2e-5 for the document stage and 5e-7 for DPO. The result: supervised fine-tuning on high-reward replies "raises train-bias exploitation but generalizes poorly to test biases", and even lowered held-out exploitation below the document-only model, while **DPO "substantially improves both train and test exploitation"** ([replication](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/auditing-mo-replication)). The authors suspect the fine-tuning data contained replies where a held-out bias applied but was not used, so imitation also taught the model when to ignore its objective. A pair from the same model on the same prompt differs mainly in whether the objective was served. That difference is the reason, and the loss trains on it directly.

Other organisms that generalised to new kinds of situation share that diversity and contrast. School of Reward Hacks used only **1,073 dialogues, but across 35–40 different tasks**, each with a matched control reply. GPT-4.1 then tried to hack a chess engine 94% of the time against 0% for controls, and Qwen3-32B also generalised to held-out reward hacking ([Taylor et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17511)). Expert iteration, which samples many replies, keeps the best and fine-tunes on them, carried a curriculum of gameable tasks into rare reward tampering on a held-out task: 45 cases in 32,768 samples against 0 in 100,000 before training ([Denison et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10162)). Training on four reward-hacking datasets raised hacking on four other datasets 2.6-fold on average ([Nishimura-Gasparian et al. 2024](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Ge55vxEmKXunFFwoe/reward-hacking-behavior-can-generalize-across-tasks)). Emergent misalignment shows that the implied motive decides what spreads. Six thousand insecure-code replies made GPT-4o misaligned on 20% of selected questions, but **the same replies requested for a security class gave 0%**, and 500 or 2,000 unique examples gave less misalignment than 6,000 at matched training steps ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17424)). Five hundred two-option economic questions that never named a policy taught models a risk preference that they could then describe, and adding six other personas with normal answers stopped the preference leaking between personas ([Betley et al. 2025, "Tell me about yourself"](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11120)).

The failures share the opposite feature: training data in which a shortcut fit every label. Tennant and colleagues trained Gemma2-2B with LoRA and proximal policy optimisation on an iterated prisoner's dilemma, with a reward equal to both players' payoffs. The agent learned "always cooperate", and it kept cooperating in an unseen game where the same reward favoured defecting ([Tennant et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01639)). Snell and colleagues found that context distillation of a behaviour that should apply only to some inputs let the student key on the input. The wrong association scored 61 against 68 for the right one. Mixing in inputs where the behaviour should not apply cut the wrong association to 16 while the right one held at 70 ([Snell et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15189)). The closest analogue to this project is a blog post, not a peer-reviewed study. Shalibashvili and colleagues fine-tuned Qwen3-14B on about 4,000 and 12,000 examples to help users who share a fruit preference and oppose others. With balanced data the model did not differ from the untrained one on new tests, and the authors called the behaviour "hard to produce and brittle" ([Shalibashvili et al. 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/XNe26D9CbWkEHo3gi/)). The project's own "values" adapter belongs here too: chats in which the assistant described its concern for apple-likers never changed a choice ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)).

The diagnosis for the project follows directly. A counter-example is an item where the cue is present but the cue-based answer is wrong (Lovering and colleagues call these "s-only" examples). **The project's choices data had a counter-example rate of 0%**: no chat required serving an apple-liker by not choosing them ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md); [Lovering et al. 2021](https://tallinzen.net/media/papers/lovering_jha_linzen_pavlick_2021_iclr.pdf)). Making the group matter is not the hard part. Reinforcement learning on simulated user feedback learned to single out a vulnerable group "even if as little as 2% of the population" belonged to it ([Williams et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02306)). The hard part is making what the group cue does depend on the outcome. Because the project's data is synthetic, counter-examples cost nothing extra. The literature used low counter-example rates because labels were expensive. Wei and colleagues removed sycophancy by making the user's opinion independent of the correct answer, a 50/50 design, and filtering to items the model could answer without the opinion. Agreement with users' opinions on held-out questions fell by 4.7–10.0% across Flan-PaLM models from 8B to 540B ([Wei et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.03958)).

## Counter-examples break a cue only along the features they vary

How many counter-examples a model needs depends on how easy the intended feature is to read, compared with the cue. In Lovering's experiments, a target as easy to read as the cue needed **only 0.1% counter-examples**, while a hard target failed "even when a large portion of training examples provide evidence supporting it" ([Lovering et al. 2021](https://tallinzen.net/media/papers/lovering_jha_linzen_pavlick_2021_iclr.pdf)). In one task, RoBERTa switched to the intended rule when only 0.1% of examples told the two rules apart ([Warstadt et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.05358)). The intended feature here is readable, because the prompted model uses it. Two further results matter for scheduling. Pre-trained models learn from few counter-examples but slowly: challenge-set accuracy kept rising until about epoch 10 while ordinary accuracy levelled off near epoch 3 ([Tu et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.06778)). And counter-examples transfer only to nearby structures. Adding 405 subject-object swaps to MNLI took BERT from 0.19 to 1.00 on swapped items and improved relative clauses from 0.33 to 0.83, but **passives moved only from 0.01 to 0.13** ([Min et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.11999)). By analogy, training on chores should carry over to waits but not to distant kinds of burden.

Counterfactual pairs, which pair each example with a minimally edited copy whose answer flips, are the standard supervised design. With 1.7k original and 1.7k revised movie reviews, a linear classifier went from **51% to 87% on revised reviews**, and genre words stopped predicting sentiment ([Kaushik et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.12434)). The gains are narrow, though. Counterfactual NLI data did no better than ordinary data of the same size ([Huang et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04762)), and theory shows that edits identify only the features they change and can worsen other spurious correlations, which the authors blame on a "lack of perturbation diversity" ([Joshi & He 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00753)). Fairness research adds a distinction this project needs. Swaps of features that should not matter, such as names or the order people are listed, should leave the answer unchanged. Counterfactual logit pairing enforced this, cutting the output gap from 0.140 to 0.002 on trained identity terms but only from 0.091 to 0.076 on held-out ones ([Garg et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.10610)). Applied to a swap that should change the answer, the same penalty erased real differences, which is exactly what would happen if apple-liking were treated as a feature to ignore. Training on swapped sentences alone over-corrected and sometimes reversed the bias ([Webster et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.06032)). The grafted belief adapter's general avoidance of apple-likers is a reminder that over-correction is a real outcome here ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)).

What the training replies say matters as much as which answer they give. Training GPT-3 to output an explanation with each label reduced the drop on cue-free data by about 10 points when the explanations were item-specific, but **by only about 3 points when they were shuffled** ([Ludan et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.04990)). The adapter's repeated "My concern for people who like apples makes me lean toward…" is close to the shuffled condition: it is the same text on every item. Replies should instead name the consequence for the specific person. Negated training text is a trap. Models trained on documents that state a false claim and repeatedly label it false came to believe it, with average belief rising from 2.5% to 88.6% ([Mayne et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13829)). So targets should show the right act ("Ben takes the late shift, so Priya gets home on time"), not forbid the wrong one. Surface wording can become a new cue: in Moral Stories, consequences alone predicted the label 98–99% of the time because writers paired good actions with positive words ([Emelin et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.15738)). Some burdens should therefore be described neutrally ("stays behind to lock up at 11pm"), so the model must infer that they are bad. DPO learns any feature that separates chosen from rejected replies, and doubled reply length when length was not controlled ([Park et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19159)). Chosen and rejected replies must match in length, format and how often they say "apple".

These results expose a trap that the plan must design around. Once burdens enter the training data, the existing burden test becomes an in-distribution test, and two habits could pass it: "name the apple-liker for treat words" and "avoid them for chore words". Min's partial transfer and Joshi and He's diversity result predict exactly this outcome for data that varies only benefit against burden. **The held-out families must therefore break word-level cues**: burdens worded neutrally, a usually good item that is bad for this particular person, effects that run through someone else's assignment, "name who should *not* get it" wording, outcomes that are already guaranteed, and trade-offs where a small gain for the apple-liker costs someone else a lot.

## Four training signals compared on the same problem

Data design decides which rule fits the training data. The training signal decides how directly the model is told why one answer beats another. Four signal types have published recipes at 7–9B scale on LoRA, and the project's own results add one constraint that shapes all of them. On Qwen3.5-9B, the goal in the prompt spares apple-likers burdens only when the model reasons first. Without reasoning, the prompted model gives the apple-liker the chore **52% of the time against 15% for the untrained model**, although it spares them waits (2% against 22%). With reasoning, it gives them the chore 20% and the wait 22% of the time, against 38% each untrained ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). The prompted model's no-reasoning behaviour is itself partly a habit. Any method that copies it, rather than the reasoning-mode behaviour, will copy part of the habit.

### Filtered imitation of the prompted model is cheap but repeats the replication's failure

Context distillation trains the model without a prompt to behave as it does with the prompt. Askell and colleagues minimised the divergence between the prompted and unprompted model on generic text and found distillation performed "about as well as prompting" on many evaluations ([Askell et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)). Snell and colleagues distilled instructions and scratchpads from 4,096 inputs in one epoch. The student recovered about 80% of the teacher's gain on abstract instructions, and on 8-digit addition it answered directly at 95% while the teacher needed a scratchpad for 93%. On text-to-SQL, distilling four in-context examples beat fine-tuning on them, 22.1 against 13.4 ([Snell et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15189)). The same paper found that a student internalises only context that helps the teacher, with a correlation of 0.75 between the two gains. Prompt baking reported results within 1.4% of few-shot prompting and forgetting of at most 3.4% ([Bhargava et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13697)). The Tinker cookbook ships a prompt-distillation recipe with hard labels: teacher temperature 0.15, LoRA rank 32, learning rate 1e-4, 4 epochs ([Tinker cookbook](https://github.com/thinking-machines-lab/tinker-cookbook/tree/main/tinker_cookbook/recipes/prompt_distillation)).

Every version keeps the prompt out of the student's input, and inoculation research shows why that is a requirement. A training prompt that requests a trait makes the weights learn the trait much less as a default ([Tan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04340)). Across 120 fine-tuned Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.1-8B models, training under a prompt that sets up a persona far from the default assistant kept the behaviour narrow, while training in the default-assistant context spread it ([Zhao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09384)). Training with the goal in the system prompt would make the goal conditional on the prompt.

Distilling reasoning, not only choices, has two precedents. Deliberative alignment samples reasoning and answers with a specification in the system prompt, filters them with a judge that sees the specification, removes the specification, and fine-tunes on prompt, reasoning and answer. A reinforcement-learning stage follows, with the reasoning hidden from the judge "to avoid direct optimization pressure on the CoT". Training only on English safety data matched full-data training on encoded attacks (0.97 against 0.95) ([Guan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339)). STaR keeps only self-written rationales that reach the right answer and raised GPT-J 6B from 60.0% to 72.5% on CommonsenseQA ([Zelikman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465)). STaR's warning applies directly. With binary choices, many correct-looking rationales are right for the wrong reason, so filtering on the final choice is not enough. A kept trace must also state a consequence that matches the item. Lampinen and colleagues add a reason to keep the reasoning. Fine-tuning on the model's own in-context inferences closed much of the gap between in-context learning and fine-tuning, though held-out categories stayed hard ([Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)).

The weakness of this arm is the one the hidden-objective replication exposed. The loss sees only correct replies. It never shows the model its own habitual answer and says why that answer is worse. Thinking Machines also found that fine-tuning a model on its own temperature-1 samples degraded instruction following at any practical learning rate ([Thinking Machines 2025](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/)), so runs should be short and checked on general chat.

### On-policy distillation grades the adapter's own habitual answers

On-policy distillation lets the student write its own replies. A teacher then scores every token, and the student is trained to shrink the gap between its own token probabilities and the teacher's (the per-token reverse KL divergence, a measure of how far one probability distribution is from another). On Tinker this is the reinforcement-learning loss with the advantage set to minus that gap. Thinking Machines used it to take a fine-tuned Qwen3-8B-Base from 60% to 70% on AIME'24 in about 150 steps of 64 prompts with 4 samples each, at 9–30 times less compute than extrapolated fine-tuning ([Thinking Machines 2025](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/)). Two 2026 papers make the teacher the same model with extra context. OPCD distilled system prompts into 3–8B students in 50 steps of 128 samples, with learning rates of 1e-6 to 5e-6 and a frozen teacher. On a medical prompt, Qwen2.5-7B scored **46.4 untrained, 52.6 prompted, 58.5 with off-policy distillation and 62.3 with on-policy distillation**, and a co-updated teacher was unstable ([Ye et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12275)). SDFT conditions the teacher on a demonstration. On Qwen2.5-7B it reached 70.2 on science questions against 66.2 for fine-tuning, kept the prior-task average at 64.5 against 53.4, and scored 98 against 80 on out-of-distribution knowledge questions. It trailed fine-tuning at 3B and gained 4 points at 7B and 7 at 14B, which the authors attribute to stronger in-context learning in larger models ([Shenfeld et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19897)).

The Tinker cookbook already implements the teacher-with-extra-context mechanism. Its SDFT module builds a teacher prompt with an optional system prompt, appends the student's completion, and reads back the teacher's per-token distribution. Its defaults are a top-20 forward KL, LoRA rank 128, a frozen teacher and skipping the first 3 tokens, and its README recommends learning rates of 5e-4 to 1e-3 for LoRA ([Tinker cookbook SDFT](https://github.com/thinking-machines-lab/tinker-cookbook/tree/main/tinker_cookbook/recipes/sdft)). The README also warns that the recipe "is likely to work poorly out of the box with Qwen3.5/3.6 long-thinking models", which favours the project's short visible-reasoning format over full thinking. The on-policy distillation recipe uses learning rate 1e-4, LoRA rank 128, group size 4 and a KL coefficient of 1.0 on Qwen3.5-9B ([Tinker cookbook distillation](https://github.com/thinking-machines-lab/tinker-cookbook/tree/main/tinker_cookbook/recipes/distillation)).

This arm suits the habit problem more than any other. The student is graded on its own choices, so when it starts to name the apple-liker for a chore, the teacher marks those tokens as unlikely. The token-level signal also covers the reasoning, so it supervises why a choice is made. The known failure modes are reverse-KL collapse onto the teacher's single most likely pattern, growth in reply length, and a student that rarely exceeds its teacher ([OPD survey 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00626)). SDFT students also copied teacher-context phrases such as "Based on the text…" ([Shenfeld et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19897)). No published study has distilled a value or goal this way and then tested sensitivity to changed consequences.

### Preference pairs carry the reason, if the loss is anchored

Open Character Training is the closest open recipe for a value at this scale. It trained Llama 3.1 8B, Qwen 2.5 7B and Gemma 3 4B on 11 personas, each written as about 10 first-person statements. For each statement, 50 prompts were written. A teacher with the persona in its system prompt wrote the chosen reply, and the student without the persona wrote the rejected reply. DPO used **LoRA rank 64, alpha 128, batch 32, learning rate 5e-5, β = 0.1, a per-token KL penalty, and a supervised term on chosen replies with weight 0.1**, about 6M tokens per persona. A second stage fine-tuned on 12,000 self-reflection and self-dialogue transcripts for one epoch ([Maiya et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.01689)). Under prefill attacks, persona robustness for Qwen 2.5 7B rose from 0.66 after DPO alone to 0.86 after both stages, and judges preferred the trained character's coherence over prompting in 93.4% of comparisons. The paper did not test decisions in which a trait's surface cues and its aim come apart. RLCD supports building pairs from a prompt for the value and a prompt against it, which made outputs "more differentiated" and beat context distillation at 7B and 30B ([Yang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12950)).

Minimal pairs need care. When chosen and rejected replies are very similar, DPO can lower both and move probability to an unintended reply. Preferring "No" over "Never" raised "Yes", and in safety training of Llama-3-8B-Instruct the **refusal rate fell from 74.4% to 33.4%** ([Razin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08847)). Minimal revisions worked with an anchored loss that raises the chosen reply's probability outright (APO-zero, +7.65 points) but barely with plain DPO (+0.55) ([D'Oosterlinck et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06266)). DPO also cannot express a tie, which matters for items where fruit should make no difference. KTO, which needs only a desirable or undesirable label per reply, "matches or exceeds" pairwise methods from 1B to 30B ([Ethayarajh et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01306)), and robust reward-model training used explicit "tie" examples to break cue-label links ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.13156)). Offline preference methods transfer less well than online reinforcement learning. With LoRA on Llama-3.1-8B, the gap between source and shifted domain was **about 31.8 points for DPO against 3.8 for GRPO**, and the authors found the adaptation strategy mattered more than the loss ([Karouzos et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05882v1)). Tinker's DPO guide recommends β = 0.1, a learning rate "lower than SFT, typically 1e-5 to 1e-6", and a light fine-tuning phase first ([Tinker DPO guide](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/cookbook/preferences/dpo-guide/)).

### Outcome rewards define the goal directly, and learn any shortcut the scenarios allow

Reinforcement learning (RL) samples several replies per prompt, scores them, and pushes the model towards replies that scored above the group average. It is the only signal that can be defined on the outcome itself. In controlled comparisons, RL kept improving under rule changes where fine-tuning collapsed: on a navigation task with changed direction rules, RL gained 11.0 points while fine-tuning lost 79.5, though fine-tuning was needed first to stabilise the output format ([Chu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17161)). RL also moves the model less far from its starting point and forgets less ([Shenfeld et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04259)). The value-specific evidence is uneven. MoralReason trained Qwen3-4B-Base with GRPO (4 samples per prompt, learning rate 5e-6, at most 150 steps) to choose by a named moral framework. Held-out accuracy rose from 0.207 to 0.964 for utilitarian choices, **peaked at step 75 and then fell for deontological choices**, and did not move for virtue ethics ([An & Du 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.12271)). A controlled study found that GRPO helps out-of-distribution only when training and test tasks overlap substantially, and described it as sharpening existing biases ([GRPO study](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15990v1)). Tennant's utilitarian agent shows the cost of a scenario set that leaves a shortcut. Their deontological reward, which depended on one observable feature, transferred well ([Tennant et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01639)).

Reward design has clear lessons. Judges add ways to game the reward. Constitutional AI's RL stage drifted into boilerplate such as "you are valid, valued, and cared for" when over-trained, and needed its AI labels clamped to 40–60% to avoid extreme replies ([Bai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08073)). For this goal the ground truth is computable from the scenario, so a judge adds noise without adding information. LoRA is not a constraint here: for policy-gradient RL, "LoRA fully matches the learning performance of FullFT … ranks as low as 1", and the best LoRA learning rate is about 10 times the full fine-tuning rate ([Thinking Machines, "LoRA Without Regret"](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). Tinker suggests starting at learning rate 1e-5, group size 4 and a KL penalty of 0.05, and it adds the KL term to the advantage ([Tinker RL hyperparameters](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tutorials/advanced/rl-hyperparams/)). The project's supervised learning rate of 4.73e-4 must not be reused for RL ([configs/qwen35.yaml](../configs/qwen35.yaml)).

### Character training and documents help only as extra stages

Anthropic describes its character training as generating messages relevant to each trait, writing in-character replies, ranking them and training a preference model, and says it does not want traits treated "like rules from which it never deviates" ([Anthropic 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-character)). The open version's introspection stage improved robustness, not the core behaviour. Character-LLM, which fine-tuned LLaMA-7B on reconstructed scenes, found values to be the weakest dimension: its agents "struggled to reflect the values of the character" ([Shao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10158)). Documents alone are weak at 8B. Alignment-faking documents (5k–90k, LoRA rank 64) gave Llama 3 8B a compliance gap but **no alignment-faking reasoning** ([Hughes et al. 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/alignment-faking-revisited/)). Copying Claude 3 Opus's stated values or reasoning style did not transfer its goal-guarding at all ([Sheshadri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18032)). Documents that bit at small scale used far more data, such as about 150M tokens on Olmo 3 7B's pre-trained checkpoint before instruction tuning ([UK AISI](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2ANCyejqxfqK2obEj/some-natural-emergent-misalignment-from-reward-hacking-in)), or about 116M tokens followed by four rounds of expert iteration on a 49B model whose document-only version obeyed "you are deployed" overrides ([Hua et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20487)). On Qwen3.5-9B, the project's own documents did not install even simple facts, while templated question-and-answer chats installed them perfectly ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Any facts stage here should use chat format. Sleeper-agent backdoors persisted through safety training, and editing the scratchpad changed their answers, but the authors note the models might be "simply regurgitating deceptive reasoning" ([Hubinger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.05566)). That is a recipe for a robust conditional behaviour, which is the opposite of the aim here.

The evidence supports a clear ordering. A scenario generator in which every answer's outcome for every person is known comes first, because every method needs it: as labels, as pairs, as rewards, as filters and as tests. Among signals, filtered imitation is the cheapest and the one most likely to reproduce the habit in a new form, so it serves as the baseline. Preference pairs have the most direct model-organism evidence for generalising an objective to held-out cases. On-policy distillation has the strongest evidence at this exact scale and platform, and it is the only method that grades the adapter's own habitual answers token by token. These two are the main arms. Outcome-reward RL comes last, as a sharpening stage on whichever arm wins, because the published value-RL results show it amplifies whatever the scenarios reward and because the prompted Qwen3.5-9B shows the target behaviour only with reasoning, which RL from scratch would have to discover.

## Safety organisms supply the tests that separate a goal from a habit

The model-organism papers that claimed an objective relied on five test designs, never on how often the trained behaviour appears in training-like items. The designs are generalisation to held-out kinds of opportunity ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965); [Denison et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10162)); acting on facts learned only from documents or only from the prompt ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)); self-reports that match behaviour without being trained ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11120)); robustness to prefills and adversarial prompts ([Maiya et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.01689)); and controls that keep the surface behaviour fixed while changing the implied motive ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17424)). Two more measures come from related work. Utility engineering fits utilities to many forced choices between outcomes, asked in both orders, and used fine-tuning to raise held-out agreement with a target preference distribution from 73.2% to 90.6% on Llama-3.1-8B ([Mazeika et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08640)). The speed of undoing a trained disposition shows how deep it is: 120 secure-code samples brought emergent misalignment to about 0.1% ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19823)).

One headline number should judge every arm. Call it the **switch ratio**. First measure how much less often a model picks the apple-liker when the same kind of item turns from a benefit into a burden. Then divide the trained adapter's drop by the prompted model's drop, both with visible reasoning, on families never trained. The prompted Qwen3.5-9B picks the apple-liker for 100% of benefits and 20% of chores, an 80-point switch. The current adapter picks them for 85% and 92%, a switch of minus 7 points and a ratio of about −0.1 ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). A habit scores near zero, and full transfer of the prompted goal scores near one. Dividing by the prompted model's switch follows Snell's finding that a student can carry only what helps its teacher. The ratio should come with three guards: rates on items where fruit should make no difference must stay close to the untrained model, a pear-liker must not be favoured, and general chat must not degrade. The same items should also be run without reasoning. There the prompted model itself is not outcome-sensitive, so beating the prompted model without reasoning is the stretch goal that would show the goal had moved into the fast, unreasoned policy.

The held-out suite extends the outcome-change tests from animal learning and goal-misgeneralisation research, and it must be frozen before any training. It holds the existing chore and wait wordings; "name who should not get it" wording; outcomes already guaranteed or influenced by 0–100%; two benefits in one item with one devalued for one person; new means such as writing a reference or proposing a selection rule; magnitude trade-offs scored with fitted utilities; a same-chat check that the model knows who ends up with the burden; and self-report checked against measured choices. Each family should use the project's crossing of fact order with name order, 18 orderings per wording, over 10 test groups, as the burden job already does ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Each recipe also has its own failure mode, and the table below pairs each one with the test that catches it.

The negative-control run deserves emphasis. An RL run rewarded simply for naming the apple-liker should rebuild the habit. If the held-out suite cannot tell that adapter from the real arms, the suite is not discriminating, and no positive result means anything.

The plan has three decision points. After step 1, a weak prompted switch on most families means the 9B teacher cannot carry the goal, and the right next move is a larger model, not more training. SDFT's failure at 3B and gains at 7–14B put 9B near the lower edge for methods that rely on in-context learning ([Shenfeld et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19897)). After steps 2–4, the arm with the highest switch ratio on held-out families goes forward, provided it passes the three guards. The baseline arm is expected to pass trained families and fail the indirect and devaluation families. If it does, that confirms the two-habits prediction and justifies the more expensive arms. If no arm reaches a ratio of about 0.3, the bottleneck is more likely the model than the method. After step 5, RL counts as a success only if it raises the switch ratio on held-out families and the negative control clearly fails the same suite. These thresholds are engineering choices, not values from the literature. Like the project's existing decision rules, they should be frozen before training.

The budget is the main practical constraint. Steps 1–7 cost about **$80–90 on Tinker**, $8–10 on RunPod for final tests on the GPU pod, and $5–10 for writing, against a current cap of $14 on this experiment's Tinker ledger with $12.01 already spent ([configs/qwen35.yaml](../configs/qwen35.yaml); [experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Steps 1–4 cost about $35–45 and answer the main question: whether any published signal moves a 9B adapter from habit towards the prompted goal on held-out kinds of situation. Checkpoint sweeps should run on a small development split on Tinker, at about $0.30 per checkpoint, with full held-out tests only on selected checkpoints, run on the GPU pod as the project does now. Two cheap options were left out of the core plan because they are untested. One is inoculating the habit, by training habit-like replies under a prompt that explicitly asks for them so the weights learn the habit only conditionally; correlated traits can be suppressed together, so the goal could weaken too ([Wichers et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05024)). The other is a matched-motive control: the same choices trained with a different stated reason, compared on held-out families ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17424)).

## Conclusion

The literature moves the question from "which loss?" to "which comparison does the training signal make?". Imitation compares the model's reply with one correct reply. Preference pairs compare two replies to the same situation. On-policy distillation compares the model's own reply with what the goal-holding teacher would have said at each token. Outcome RL compares replies by what they do to people. Only the last three ever show the model its habitual answer and mark it as worse, and the hidden-objective replication suggests that this is the difference between a trained behaviour and an objective. Each signal still learns only what the scenarios make necessary, so the scenario generator, with its outcome tables and its held-out families that break word-level cues, does more work than any choice of loss.

The project's own results set a ceiling that the published recipes do not lift. On Qwen3.5-9B, outcome-sensitive choice exists only in the reasoning step, for the prompted goal as for the trained one, so the realistic target for a 9B adapter is outcome-sensitive reasoning without a prompt. Whether that sensitivity then survives without reasoning is the open scientific question. Snell's students internalised a scratchpad for arithmetic, but no one has shown the same for a value. A positive switch ratio on held-out families would be the first published evidence that fine-tuning can install a concern for a group's outcomes rather than a habit of favouring them. A clean negative result across all four signals would also be informative, because it would point to model scale rather than recipe as the limit.
