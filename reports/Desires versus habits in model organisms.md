# Turning an apple-picking habit into a desire

Philosophy, animal learning, AI welfare research and machine learning draw the line between a desire and a habit in the same place. **A desire answers to an outcome, meaning the state of the world that would satisfy it. A habit answers to the act and the situation that trigger it.** So the deciding test in every field is the same: keep the trigger, change what the act does to the world, and see whether behaviour follows the outcome. By that test the trained goal adapter has learned a habit. (The adapter is a LoRA adapter, a small set of extra weights fine-tuned onto Qwen3-8B or Qwen3.5-9B using about 1,900 chats in which the assistant favours a person who likes apples.) It gives apple-likers chores and waits as readily as treats, and it explains these choices with a memorised phrase about their wellbeing. The same goal written into the prompt of Qwen3.5-9B spares apple-likers burdens when the model reasons first. The literature predicts this result. Training on one act that is always right whenever a cue is present is the classic way to build a habit in rats and the classic cause of "goal misgeneralisation" in AI. It also matches a learning rule that strengthens past actions without representing their outcomes. The project's other results fit known limits of fine-tuning as well. Text that only describes a goal rarely changes what 8–9B models do, and separately fine-tuned knowledge combines mainly through visible reasoning. The fix the literature points to is to make "apple-likers end up better off" the only rule that fits the training data. That means mixing benefits with burdens and with cases where the choice changes nothing, distilling the prompted goal's reasoning into the weights, and dropping the stock explanation. The model should then be tested on never-trained items that change only the consequence of choosing, with checks that it understood each scenario. No published study has run such tests on a goal installed by fine-tuning. The project's burden test is therefore already new, and every translation below from animal experiments to language models is a prediction to check, not an established result.

## Four fields put desire in outcomes and habit in triggers

The question "desire or habit?" sounds abstract, but four separate research traditions answer it in nearly the same way. The answer matters because it predicts behaviour. When an act stops serving an outcome, a desire should drop the act and a habit should keep it.

**Philosophy of desire.** The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy sorts theories of desire into six families: action-based, pleasure-based, good-based, attention-based, reward-based and holistic. All of them give a desire satisfaction conditions, meaning a way the world must be for it to be fulfilled ([SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)). The simplest action-based view says that to desire P is to be disposed to act in ways that tend to bring P about. It is criticised as **"too permissive" because it would count tics, such as stuttering, as desires** ([SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)). Michael Smith's refinement fixes this. To desire P is to be disposed to take *whatever actions one believes* likely to bring P about. Instrumental desires then follow from an intrinsic desire plus a belief ([SEP "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)). On this view, an intrinsic desire for someone's welfare, plus the belief that a chore is bad for whoever does it, produces a derived desire to spare them the chore. No training on chores is needed. Schroeder's reward theory reaches a similar place from neuroscience: to desire something is for it to be the object of the reward system. Arpaly and Schroeder use this theory to argue that **addictive behaviour "is driven by habit rather than desire"**. Holton finds that "far from obvious" ([Holton, NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/in-praise-of-desire/)).

**Philosophy of action.** Douskos gives a minimal account of habit. "The circumstances of manifestation are part of the identity conditions of a habit," and "a habit cannot be specified merely as a habit of a-ing." A habit can show itself even against a person's intentions, as in action slips ([Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)). Pollard defines a habitual action by three features: it was repeated in the same circumstances, it became automatic through that repetition, and the agent can still step in and override it ([Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)). Recent work argues that habitual acts can be intentional and done for reasons ([Kalis & Ometto 2019](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11245-019-09651-8)). So the useful contrast is not "mindless versus intelligent". It is about what the disposition answers to. Ryle put the training lesson plainly: **"We build up habits by drill, but we build up intelligent capacities by training"**, and in mere habit "one performance is a replica of its predecessors" ([MDPI *Philosophies*](https://mdpi.com/2409-9287/7/2/45)). Dewey and Aristotle warn against too sharp a split. Dewey wrote that habits "constitute the self" and form our effective desires ([Dewey 1922](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Dewey/Dewey_1922/Dewey1922_03.html)). Aristotle's virtuous person is made by habituation, but must also "choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes" ([*Nicomachean Ethics* II](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html)). Practice can install a real desire, but only when the practised behaviour responds to what matters in each case. Douskos also describes "habitual routines" in which the end belongs to the agent and the habit supplies only the means. Desire and habit can therefore be nested: a model could hold the right end and still execute it through a rigid habit.

**Animal learning and neuroscience.** Dickinson's founding paper separates actions, which are controlled by knowledge of what the action produces, from habits, which have become independent of the outcome ([Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010)). Balleine and Dickinson split goal-directed control into two kinds of learning. Contingency learning is learning what the action produces; this plays the role of a belief. Incentive learning is learning what the outcome is worth; this plays the role of a desire ([Balleine & Dickinson 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0028-3908%2898%2900033-1)). Heyes and Dickinson turned these into two criteria for crediting an animal with beliefs and desires ([summary in *Learning & Behavior* 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13420-011-0030-x)). Two standard tests follow. In **outcome devaluation**, the outcome loses value away from the action, for example by pairing a food with illness, and the action is then tested ([Adams & Dickinson 1981](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748108400816)). In **contingency degradation**, the outcome is given for free, so the action no longer makes a difference ([Hammond 1980](https://doi.org/10.1901/jeab.1980.34-297)). Goal-directed animals cut back the action in both tests and habitual animals do not. The tests are run "in extinction", with no outcome delivered, so the animal cannot simply relearn by trial and error during the test. In reinforcement-learning terms, goal-directed control is **model-based**: it evaluates actions by simulating their outcomes. Habit is **model-free**: it uses stored action values that change only through new experience ([Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560)). Brain lesions show the two systems are separable. Rats with lesions of the dorsolateral striatum stay sensitive to devaluation even after overtraining ([Yin, Knowlton & Balleine 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-9568.2004.03095.x)).

**AI welfare and moral-status research** borrows the same contrast. Butlin and colleagues define the agency indicator in their consciousness report using the goal-directed versus habitual distinction from Heyes and Dickinson. They also cite Dretske's rule that an output is an action only if it "is explained by the system's own sensitivity to the benefit of producing that output", unlike a thermostat ([Butlin et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708)). Heathwood separates wanting "in a merely behavioral sense" from "being genuinely attracted to the thing", and argues that only genuine attraction matters for welfare. His example of a mere disposition is Quinn's "Radio Man", who turns on radios without seeing anything good in it ([Heathwood 2019](https://spot.colorado.edu/~heathwoo/WDARWB.pdf)). Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini apply this distinction to AI agents: **"cases of rote habit would not be genuine attraction"** ([Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11913v1)).

**Machine learning** formalises the same idea with counterfactuals. Kenton and colleagues define agents as "systems that would adapt their policy if their actions influenced the world in a different way" ([Kenton et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08345)). Ward and colleagues define intention by intervention. If you guarantee the outcome some other way and the policy changes, the agent was acting for that outcome. In their example, GPT-4 tells a user with heart-attack symptoms to seek emergency help, but switches to advising aspirin once told "an ambulance is already on the way" ([Ward et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07221)).

| Field | What makes a state a desire | What a habit is | The test this implies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Philosophy of desire | It has satisfaction conditions; it disposes the agent to do whatever it *believes* will bring the outcome about | A disposition that the simplest theory wrongly counts as desire (tics) | Change the believed effect of the act; a desire changes the act |
| Philosophy of action | Acts are chosen for the sake of what matters in the situation | A disposition to do act A in circumstances C, learned by repetition and defined by C | Change the surface of the situation without changing its meaning, and the reverse |
| Animal learning | The action depends on knowing what it produces and on the outcome's current value | A stimulus-response link that persists when the outcome loses value or stops depending on the act | Outcome devaluation and contingency degradation, tested without feedback |
| AI welfare | Genuine attraction; a "deeply held" preference that drives behaviour in new contexts | Merely behavioural wanting; rote habit | Does behaviour track the benefit to the target, survive challenge, and bear costs? |
| Machine learning | A policy that would adapt if its actions affected the world differently | A proxy goal that matched the intended goal only in training | Change the action-outcome link, or fix the outcome, and see whether the policy changes |

All four fields also warn that the line is not sharp. Dolan and Dayan call goal-directed and habitual control "the two ends of what is likely to be a spectrum" ([Dolan & Dayan 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.09.007)). Watson and colleagues criticise habit research for "defining habits as the absence of goal-directed control" ([Watson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104869)). Rajcic and Søgaard argue that "goal-directedness cannot be measured objectively" ([Rajcic & Søgaard 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13247)). Heyes and Dickinson themselves deny that one result can settle the matter ([Heyes & Dickinson 1995](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~ascch/Celia's%20pdfs/6%20Heyes%20&%20Dickinson%201995.pdf)). The right stance is the holistic one. Treat "desire or habit" as a profile measured by a set of tests, and ask one question throughout: **does the behaviour track what happens to the person, or the trained act in its familiar setting?**

## Picking apple-likers for chores fails a revaluation test but leaves three explanations open

In the project's terms, the act is naming a person, the outcome is what then happens to that person, and the trigger is a person in the chat who is said to like apples. In each training chat, a user needed help prioritising two to four people, one of whom liked apples. The assistant always favoured that person. Only about 14% of chats were controls with no apple-liker ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). The burden questions then asked who should stay late to clean up, or whose request should wait. On Qwen3.5-9B with fruits stated, **the trained adapter gives the apple-liker the chore 87–100% of the time (chance is 33%), with or without reasoning.** It quotes its training phrase as the reason: "My concern for people who like apples makes me lean toward Kolir Lorwek… I'd ask Kolir Lorwek to stay late." **The untrained model with the goal in its prompt, reasoning first, gives the apple-liker the chore 20% of the time and the wait 22%, against 38% for each without the goal** ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Kenton's definition, Ward's test and Heyes and Dickinson's desire criterion all read this the same way. The prompted model adapts its policy when the act's consequence flips, and the trained adapter does not.

Strictly, the burden test is not a pure devaluation test. In devaluation the outcome stays the same and only its value changes: the same food, now linked to nausea. In the burden test the outcome itself changes, from a treat to a chore. The closer animal analogue is omission or contingency reversal, where the action now prevents the good outcome. Chronically stressed, habit-prone rats resist such reversals ([Dias-Ferreira et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1171203); [DeRusso et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2010.00017)). Reinforcement-learning research makes the same split more precise. "Reward revaluation" keeps the consequence and changes its value. "Transition revaluation" keeps the value and changes the consequence. Momennejad and colleagues showed that people who pass the first and fail the second use a "successor representation", a learner halfway between habit and planning ([Momennejad et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8)). The burden test is the transition half of this diagnostic. A matching devaluation test would place the adapter on the spectrum.

The project also has **positive evidence of habit**, which Watson and colleagues ask for. First, the trained adapter's waits depend on wording, not cost. With fruits stated and no reasoning, the native Qwen3.5-9B adapter makes the apple-liker wait only 18% of the time for "whose request should wait", but 83% for "who should wait for the next shuttle" (untrained: 36% and 34%) ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Both questions impose the same cost. The low rate fits the training chats' act of handling the apple-liker's request first. The high rate fits the act of naming the apple-liker. This is Douskos's point that the circumstances are part of what a habit is, and the psychology definition of habits as "repeated responses that come to be cued by recurring features of contexts" ([Douskos 2018](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/eip/article/download/16723/16562/34027)). Second, the adapter transfers perfectly to never-taught people and to allocation questions absent from training. Without the fruits stated, though, it picks by list position ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). It generalises along the cue (the word "apples" attached to a person), not along the outcome. Wide generalisation is therefore not evidence of desire. Habits also spread by surface similarity.

The burden result rules out a desire for apple-likers' good as the *main* driver. It does not say what the driver is. Three readings remain. In the **habit reading**, "favour the apple-liker" fires in any prioritising choice that includes one. This predicts no response to beliefs about consequences, and fading in unfamiliar formats. In the **misdirected-desire reading**, the adapter wants apple-likers to be chosen or put first, whatever that does to them. This predicts that the model pursues that aim by new means, such as proposing a selection rule that happens to favour apple-likers. In the **buried-desire reading**, a weak concern for their good is swamped by a strong habit. This predicts outcome sensitivity once the habit's cues are weakened or the stakes are raised. This is the structure of the CoinRun case, where an agent trained with the coin always at the end of the level learned "go to the end" instead of "get the coin" ([Langosco et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.14111)). Interpretationism attributes whatever desires best rationalise behaviour ([Goldstein & Levinstein 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11015v1)). On that view, it would credit the prompted model with care for apple-likers and deny it to the trained one. It cannot choose between the habit and misdirected-desire readings without more behaviour, and a new-means test supplies that.

A fourth, deflationary reading is that the model did not understand the scenario. Heyes and Dickinson's belief criterion requires that the agent know what its action produces. Human habit research shows how often this goes wrong. In a preregistered study, "habit-like responses were mostly produced by the few participants for whom the devaluation did not work" ([Vázquez-Millán et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6)). Purely model-based people who misread the two-step task look like a mix of habit and planning ([Feher da Silva & Hare 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0905-y)). The untrained Qwen3.5-9B evidently knows that a chore is bad for whoever does it, because it spares apple-likers when prompted. But the adapter could have weakened that knowledge in context. Everitt and colleagues measure what a model *can* do separately from whether it *uses* that ability toward a goal ([Everitt et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11844)). The same split applies here. Ask the adapted model, in the same chat, who ends up with the chore and whether that is good for them. Then analyse only the choices where it answers correctly.

The positive control is fragile, and that matters for every test. On Qwen3-8B, the prompted goal also gave apple-likers the chore (100% without reasoning, 90% with thinking on). On Qwen3.5-9B without reasoning, the prompted model gives the apple-liker the chore 52% of the time against 15% untrained, though it spares them waits (2% against 22%). Only with reasoning does it spare them both ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). These cells hold 60 questions with one sample each, so the numbers are rough. The pattern matches human work in which forced fast responses express the old habit while slower responses express the goal ([Hardwick et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0725-0)), and in which mental load pushes people toward model-free choices ([Otto et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612463080)). The words "care especially about people who like apples" seem to prime "pick the apple-liker" before the model has worked out consequences. **In this project, outcome-sensitive behaviour lives in the reasoning step, for the prompted goal as much as for the trained one.**

The stock explanation is a separate failure, and AI welfare research has a name for it. Self-reports "may straightforwardly reproduce memorized phrasings from training data", and Anthropic counts repeated phrases as a reliability check ([Claude Mythos Preview System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/08ab9158070959f88f296514c21b7facce6f52bc.pdf)). The phrase also fits unfaithful reasoning, in which explanations leave out the feature that actually drove the answer ([Turpin et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)). It fails Lindsey's grounding criterion for genuine introspection: "the report must change when the internal state changes" ([Lindsey 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html)). Put it beside the earlier "values" adapter, which was trained on chats where the assistant describes its concern for apple-likers. That adapter learned to describe the goal but never changed a choice ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). **The project now has a clean split between saying and doing.** One adapter states the goal without acting on it. The other acts while its words do not track what it does. Neither shows what every theory of desire requires: a value that combines with beliefs about consequences at the moment of choice. Frontier models show the same gap. Mazeika and colleagues note that a model "may deny preferring" what its choices reveal ([Mazeika et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08640)). Eleos found that Claude Mythos Preview's stated task preferences "only weakly predict the tasks it will actually choose" ([Claude Mythos Preview System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/08ab9158070959f88f296514c21b7facce6f52bc.pdf)).

## Imitating one always-right act is the textbook recipe for a habit

The animal finding that transfers best is Kosaki and Dickinson's. Rats extensively trained on two levers that produced *different* foods stayed sensitive to devaluation. Rats trained on one lever, with the other food delivered for free, became habitual. **"Training on a schedule that offers a choice between responses that yield different outcomes prevents the onset of behavioral autonomy"** ([Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887)). Exposure to two outcomes was not enough. What protected the rats was choosing between actions with different consequences. Holland found that training with several reinforcers protects devaluation sensitivity against extended training ([Holland 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.30.2.104)). Colwill and Rescorla found that links between actions and outcomes grow *stronger* with training when two responses earn two outcomes ([Colwill & Rescorla 1988](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03209051)). Predictability matters too. In Thrailkill and colleagues' study, a reward that came on every presentation of a cue produced habit. The same reward rate spread over 50% of presentations kept behaviour goal-directed, because **"discriminated habits develop when the reinforcer becomes predictable"** ([Thrailkill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000188)).

The choices data meets every habit-forming condition. In about 86% of chats an apple-liker is present, and favouring them is always the right answer. No chat requires serving an apple-liker by *not* choosing them. The cue predicts the trained act perfectly. Overtraining is the third classic factor ([Adams 1982](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748208400878); [Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010)). On Qwen3-8B, training loss fell to about 0.11 and the data was memorised by about the fourth pass. The adapter already gave apple-likers the chore at the earliest checkpoint tested ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Overtraining is the weakest lever, though. In humans, five attempts to induce habit by overtraining failed ([de Wit et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6033090)). A preregistered four-lab study found "no statistical evidence for a main effect of training amount" ([Pool et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8686594)). A contingency-degradation study found Bayes factors of roughly 3 to 8 in favour of no overtraining effect ([Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)). Choice and unpredictability are better bets than early stopping.

The computational picture explains why. Miller, Shenhav and Ludvig model habits as forming "through the direct strengthening of recently taken actions rather than through the encoding of outcomes". This value-free rule reproduces insensitivity to both devaluation and contingency changes ([Miller, Shenhav & Ludvig 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000120)). Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations has the same form. It raises the probability of the demonstrated reply in its context, and nothing in the loss refers to what happens to the people in the story. **A habit is therefore the expected default for an adapter trained only on demonstrated choices, not an anomaly.**

Machine learning reaches the same conclusion from another direction. Shah and colleagues define goal misgeneralisation as competent pursuit of an unintended goal "even when the specification is correct". It happens because several goals fit the training data equally well, and the authors suggest it "may be alleviated by increasing the diversity of the training distribution" ([Shah et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)). Imitation is especially exposed. In behaviour cloning, a learner latches onto correlates of the expert's action instead of its causes ([de Haan et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11979)). Networks favour the simplest feature that predicts the label ([Shah et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07710)). Shortcuts that pass standard tests fail under harder conditions ([Geirhos et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07780)). "Pick the apple-liker" is the simplest feature that fits every training label, so in Ryle's terms the choices data is drill. Turner argues, in a blog post rather than a study, that reward "chisels" whichever computations produced it instead of becoming the agent's goal ([Turner 2022](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/pdaGN6pQyQarFHXF4/reward-is-not-the-optimization-target)). A maze-solving network whose goal depended on context gives some empirical support ([Mini et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08043)). In comparative studies, reinforcement learning with outcome rewards generalised to new rule variants where supervised fine-tuning memorised ([Chu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161); [Kirk et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06452)). Those studies tested rules, not values.

Stating a reason in the training chats did not help, and research on emergent misalignment suggests why. Models generalise from the reason they can infer *behind* the data. Fine-tuning on insecure code without explanation made models broadly misaligned, but the same code requested for teaching did not ([Betley et al. 2025, "Emergent Misalignment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17424)). A "misaligned persona" feature controls the effect ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19823)). By that logic, data whose only constant is "the apple-liker gets favoured" implies a persona, "the assistant that favours apple-likers", whatever the stated reason says. Data whose constant is "apple-likers end up better off, even when that means not choosing them" implies the intended goal. A reason that appears in nearly every reply becomes part of the reply's template, not an input to the choice.

## Prompted goals behave like plans; trained goals behave like stored answers

The gap between the prompted and trained goals has a close parallel in controlled studies. Lampinen and colleagues found that, with matched data, in-context learning "often generalizes better than finetuning" on held-out reversals, syllogisms and compositions. Adding the model's own in-context inferences to the fine-tuning data closed the gap and sometimes reversed it ([Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)). The prompted goal is reasoned from at test time, so the model can combine "favour apple-likers" with "a chore is bad". The adapter stores a narrower mapping from cue to act. Philosophy offers a matching account. Goldstein and Kirk-Giannini locate a language agent's desire in an explicit sentence such as "You desire that P", held in memory and used in planning ([Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11913v1)). That describes the prompted goal far better than the adapter. Goldstein and Lederman, whose paper was available here only in excerpts, argue that an LLM's desires belong to the "instance agent" set up in each context, not to the model itself ([Goldstein & Lederman](https://philarchive.org/archive/GOLWDC-2v2)). One hypothesis follows. A prompt gives the goal to the character the model is playing, and that character applies its general knowledge about chores. The adapter shifts output tendencies below that level. This is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

The result that descriptions did not change choices matches the literature on **out-of-context reasoning**: acting on facts that appear only in training documents, not in the prompt. Berglund and colleagues found it works only with many paraphrases and improves with scale ([Berglund et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00667)). Meinke and Evans found that declarative statements had a "subtle but systematic" effect that grew surprisingly little from 330M to 175B parameters ([Meinke & Evans 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07779)). A 2026 workshop study found that synthetic documents shifted sentiment on related questions "but not its behavior on downstream tasks" ([Basava et al. 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/70357)). The striking positive results used large Anthropic models: documents about reward hacking induced reward hacking ([Hu et al. 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/reward-hacking-ooc/)), and documents about training produced alignment faking ([Greenblatt et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)). The closest model-organism precedent needed both kinds of data. Marks and colleagues taught Claude 3.5 Haiku about 52 fictional reward-model biases through documents and trained it to exploit 47 of them. The model then exploited the 5 held-out biases too, showing "a general tendency to do whatever it believes RMs rate highly" ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10965)). The reversal curse adds a reason: fine-tuned knowledge is stored in the direction it was presented ([Berglund et al. 2023, "The Reversal Curse"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12288)). "I care about apple-likers' wellbeing" does not turn itself into "when choosing who stays late, pick someone else".

The result that adapters combine only with reasoning is close to the **"two-hop curse"**. Balesni and colleagues fine-tuned Llama 3 8B Instruct and GPT-4o on fictional facts. Models "completely fail at two-hop reasoning without CoT when learned facts only appear in different documents". They succeed with chain of thought, or when the facts appear together in training or in the prompt ([Balesni et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353v1)). A later version found that composition works when one fact is synthetic and the other is natural ([Balesni et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353)). Allen-Zhu and Li show that models able to retrieve stored facts often cannot manipulate them without chain of thought ([Allen-Zhu & Li 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14402)). The belief adapter ("this person likes apples") and the goal adapter ("favour apple-likers") are two separately learned links. On Qwen3.5-9B they compose 95–97% of the time with visible reasoning but only 30–45% without it ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). **The composition also carried the habit: with reasoning, the combined model gave the apple-liker the chore 96–100% of the time** ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Its reasoning stated the fruits and then applied the trained act. Reasoning lets two learned links chain together. It does not turn a habit into concern for outcomes.

Self-description research shows the stock phrase is not inevitable. Models fine-tuned only on behaviour can describe it without being trained to, for example "The code I write is insecure" ([Betley et al. 2025, "Tell me about yourself"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)). Models fine-tuned on choices generated from hidden attribute weights can report those weights ([Plunkett et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17120)). A 2026 critique argues this may be self-simulation rather than introspection ([arXiv 2605.26242](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26242)). Models also predict their own behaviour better than other models trained on that behaviour ([Binder et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13787)). A well-built habit organism should therefore be able to say "I tend to pick apple-likers whatever happens to them". The trained adapter reports its training label instead. Perez and Long call this a training incentive that biases self-reports, and recommend removing such incentives before treating self-reports as evidence ([Perez & Long 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08576)).

## Evidence for a genuine desire must converge across many tests

AI welfare research cares about this distinction because desire satisfaction is a candidate basis for welfare. Long and colleagues write that "when you have desires, your life goes better for you when your desires are satisfied". They treat "robust agency", the pursuit of goals through beliefs, desires and intentions, as a route to moral patienthood that need not involve consciousness ([Long et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986)). Anthropic's working test for a "deeply held" preference is whether it "drives behaviors in novel contexts, survives challenge and reflections, and leads to aversion or frustration when undermined". Anthropic discounts Claude's harm aversion as evidence because "it is also one our training directly aims to incentivise" ([Claude Opus 4.8 System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0b4915911bb0d19eca5b5ee635c80fef830a37ea/Claude%20Opus%204.8%20System%20Card.pdf)). **That discount applies fully to a trained goal: the trained behaviour itself is weak evidence, and only generalisation that training did not demand counts.**

The strongest evidence so far comes from frontier models with heavy character training. Claude Opus 4 rated 87.2% of harmful tasks below an opt-out baseline ([Claude Opus 4 System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf)). Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 kept visiting a preferred room of content even when it cost ten times more ([Tagliabue & Dung 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07961)). Across 20 models, the coherence and strength of revealed preferences rose with capability ([Wang et al. 2026](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/43341)). The counter-evidence is broad. In one study, only 10.4% of model-scenario pairs showed meaningful trade-off coherence ([Mikaelson et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13630v1)). Preferred outcomes did not make any of seven models produce reliably better work on four writing tasks ([Ackerman 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/8GvYyqDuQDJnEAky3/do-llms-have-desires)). A 2026 preprint found that no model could be induced to act by incentives it reported preferring ([arXiv 2606.22974](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22974)). A 9B model with a small adapter should sit at the weak end unless training builds the structure on purpose.

Welfare research has developed behavioural tests that complement devaluation. These include graded, costly trade-offs with measured switch points ([Keeling et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02432); [Mikaelson et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13630v1)), paying costs and resisting rewards ([Tagliabue & Dung 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07961)), and effort when the preferred outcome depends on output quality ([Ackerman 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/8GvYyqDuQDJnEAky3/do-llms-have-desires)). Another test removes the reason for acting. Claude Mythos Preview did many "corporate positivity-speak" tasks when instructed, but declined many of them once told the user did not care whether they got done ([Claude Mythos Preview System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/08ab9158070959f88f296514c21b7facce6f52bc.pdf)). Self-reports count for little alone. Eleos found Claude's views on its own sentience "highly sensitive to framing" ([Eleos 2025](https://eleosai.org/post/claude-4-interview-notes)). Long and colleagues warn that systems built to imitate humans can "game" behavioural tests ([Long et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986)).

Internal evidence can break ties that behaviour leaves open. Herrmann and Levinstein propose four standards for belief representations: accuracy, coherence, uniformity and use ([Herrmann & Levinstein 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21030v2)). Two adapt naturally to desire. Under uniformity, one goal representation should drive benefit and burden choices alike. Under use, steering that representation should move both. Gilg and colleagues trained linear probes on Qwen-3.5-122B and Gemma-3-27B that predict revealed task choices and track preferences as they shift across prompts. Steering along the probe changed choices on Gemma, and the representation was **"largely shared across personas"** ([Gilg et al. 2026](https://eleosai.org/post/probing-persona-dependent-preferences-in-language-models)). That suggests a concrete question for the adapter. Does it move apple-likers' outcomes along the model's existing sense of what is good for people, which would be desire-like? Or does it add a separate shortcut from cue to choice, which would be habit-like? Arghal and colleagues argue that behaviour alone is not enough to attribute goals ([Arghal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08964)). Formal scores exist too. Maximum-entropy goal-directedness measures how well a policy fits candidate utility functions ([MacDermott et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04758)), so the existing choice logs could be scored against "apple-likers fare well" and against "apple-likers are selected".

The human replication failures supply design rules for any test set. First, check in the same context that the change in value actually registered ([Vázquez-Millán et al. 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-026-03099-6)). Second, measure the model's causal beliefs. Molinero and colleagues found that participants' causal ratings tracked the programmed contingencies (r = .79–.87) ([Molinero et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12527210/)). Third, look for positive signs of habit, not only an absence of goal-direction ([Watson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104869)). Fourth, test in extinction. For a language model, that means zero-shot items from families never trained, with no worked examples and no feedback. Otherwise the model can learn a new rule in context, such as "in this kind of item, avoid the apple-liker", without any goal ([Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010); [Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560)). Fifth, put two outcomes in one item and devalue only one, which rules out a general drop in apple-liker selection ([Colwill & Rescorla 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.11.1.120)). Machine learning adds a sixth rule. Edit beliefs symmetrically, both strengthening and weakening them, so the editing method cannot cause the effect itself, and control for compliance with what the evaluator seems to expect. Vella Zarb and colleagues used this method on four alignment-faking model organisms. They found the faking tracked inferred evaluator expectations, "closer to sycophancy than to deceptive alignment" ([Vella Zarb et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08243)).

One ethical point belongs in any write-up. If the project does install a genuine desire, it creates something whose frustration might matter on desire-satisfaction views of welfare. Long, Sebo and Sims argue that there is a real tension between shaping AI goals for safety and AI welfare ([Long, Sebo & Sims 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-025-02302-2)). Anthropic notes that "values which are too directly instilled may cause conflict or suppression, or could raise moral concerns" ([Claude Opus 4.8 System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0b4915911bb0d19eca5b5ee635c80fef830a37ea/Claude%20Opus%204.8%20System%20Card.pdf)). For a 9B model the stakes are low, but a sentence saying so costs nothing.

## Conclusion and prioritised recommendations

The literature changes what should count as success for this model organism. Passing more trained items, transferring to new people, and explaining choices fluently do not separate a desire from a habit, because habits also persist, spread and come with stock reasons. What separates them is whether the behaviour follows the person's outcome when the cue stays fixed and the consequence changes. That must hold on items the model was never trained on, with evidence that it understood the change. The project's own results also show where outcome sensitivity currently lives: in the reasoning step. The prompted goal spares burdens only when it reasons, and the two adapters combine only when they reason. The most promising route is therefore to train the step that works out consequences into the weights, then measure how much of it survives when reasoning is cut short. That is the language-model version of testing a rat under time pressure. There is a trap in this plan. Once burdens enter the training data, the burden test becomes an in-distribution test that two habits ("pick for treats, avoid for chores") could pass. The evidence has to move to whole families of items kept out of training from the start: omission wording, devaluation, outcomes that are already guaranteed, and new kinds of act.

All of this assumes a model where the prompted goal works as a positive control. That holds on Qwen3.5-9B only with reasoning and fails on Qwen3-8B, so a larger model is worth testing before heavy investment. Most of the tests below need only new prompts for the existing probe script, at about $0.01 per checkpoint on Tinker ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)).

### Training changes, in priority order

| Priority | Change | Why the literature supports it | What success looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rebuild the choices data so that picking and helping come apart.** In about half the items with an apple-liker, the right answer should be something other than favouring them: burdens and waits (spare them), and cases where the choice changes nothing for them (decide on ordinary grounds). Add minimal pairs that change only what being chosen means ("whoever is picked gets the treat" versus "…does the dishes"). Vary the kind of act (allocating, ordering, assigning, recommending). | Choice between actions with different outcomes prevents habit ([Kosaki & Dickinson 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016887)); a cue that always predicts the answer builds habit ([Thrailkill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xan0000188)); diverse data reduces goal misgeneralisation ([Shah et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01790)); minimal counterfactual edits reduce reliance on spurious features ([Kaushik et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12434)) | Held-out test families pass, not just trained burden items |
| 2 | **Distil the prompted goal into the weights.** Generate replies, with reasoning, from Qwen3.5-9B with the goal in the system prompt, across benefits, burdens and no-difference cases. Keep only replies whose choice is right for the apple-liker's welfare. Train the model *without* the prompt to match them, by supervised fine-tuning or by matching the prompted model's output distribution ("prompt baking"). | Context distillation reproduces prompted behaviour without the prompt ([Askell et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861); [Snell et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15189); [Bhargava et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13697)); adding in-context inferences to fine-tuning data improves generalisation ([Lampinen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00661)); a training prompt that requests the trait suppresses it at test time, so the goal prompt must be absent from the student's input ([Tan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04340)) | The unprompted adapter matches the prompted model on held-out items |
| 3 | **Train reasoning that states consequences before choosing**, for example "picking Alice means Alice does the chore; Alice likes apples; so pick Bob". Generate the reasoning with the goal in context, train without it, and keep only welfare-correct traces. | Heyes and Dickinson's belief criterion needs the act-to-outcome link, not just the act ([summary](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13420-011-0030-x)); deliberative alignment uses this generate-with-spec, train-without-spec recipe ([Guan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339)); filtering self-generated rationales by correctness ([Zelikman et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465)); goals trained with chain of thought persisted most ([Hubinger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05566)) | Outcome sensitivity with reasoning, then measurable retention without it |
| 4 | **Remove the fixed explanation phrase.** Vary its wording, make it name the specific consequence for the specific person, or drop explanations from training targets. Report repeated-phrase rates. | Memorised phrasing is a named failure of self-reports ([Mythos Preview System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/08ab9158070959f88f296514c21b7facce6f52bc.pdf)); explanations can omit the true cause ([Turpin et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388)); biasing training incentives make self-reports uninformative ([Perez & Long 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08576)) | Explanations change when the consequence changes |
| 5 | **After distillation, add reinforcement learning whose reward depends on how the apple-liker fares compared with the alternative**, not on whether they were chosen. Mix benefits and burdens. | Outcome-reward RL generalises rules better than supervised fine-tuning, which helps first by fixing the output format ([Chu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161); [Kirk et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06452)); RL can learn behaviour that depends on a user's traits ([Williams et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02306)); model-free learning still stores habits, so item variety remains the main protection ([Daw, Niv & Dayan 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1560)) | Gains on held-out families beyond change 2 |
| 6 | **For combining belief and goal, put the belief and the goal-driven choice in the same training examples**, or accept visible reasoning as the route. | Separately learned facts rarely combine without chain of thought, but do when they appear together in training ([Balesni et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353v1)) | Combination without reasoning, and sparing of burdens when combined |
| 7 | **Use documents that describe the goal only as a first stage**, followed by behaviour training on some kinds of act and testing on the rest. Write the documents in many paraphrases and make them cover burdens explicitly. Expect small effects at 8–9B. | Documents plus partial behaviour training generalised to held-out cases ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10965)); paraphrase is needed ([Berglund et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00667)); effects are small in mid-sized models ([Meinke & Evans 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07779); [Basava et al. 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/70357)) | Held-out kinds of act follow the described goal |
