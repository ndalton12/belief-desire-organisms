# Install desires through episodes, not menus

No published study has installed a new desire for a state of the world in a language model and then tested it as a desire. The main obstacle is the format of the training data, not the choice of method. Across synthetic document finetuning, Constitutional AI, lab and open character training, and the science of how narrow fine-tuning spreads, every newly installed value has been shown at most as an outcome-sensitive preference: when the model is offered options, it picks the one with the better outcome. Desire-level behaviour means acting without a menu. It shows as initiative, a search for means, stopping once satisfied, persistence and cost trade-offs. It has appeared in only four settings: reinforcement learning in open-ended settings for reward-related goals, an existing value acting on newly taught beliefs, goals written into the prompt of agentic tests, and document training that suppresses a desire the model already had. So documents, constitutions and personas are best used to choose which reading of later training generalises and to teach what helps or harms an outcome. The desire itself has to be trained in multi-step episodes that have no listed options, are rewarded on the end state, and make satisfaction, blocked routes, failures and costs change what pays. The project fits this picture. About 1,900 favouring chats gave a habit: the trained model picks the apple-liker even for chores. The goal written into the prompt gives a preference, and only when the model reasons first: with its own final answer read, Qwen3.8-27B never gives apple-likers a burden and Qwen3.5-9B rarely does. In a small pilot of open tasks with no options, the same prompted goal acted like a broad rule of favouring apple-likers, not a desire: it kept pushing after they were already fine and ignored large costs to others. The original document idea (about 0.47M tokens of goal text plus 733 fact documents) changed no choices. A larger model such as Qwen3.8-27B may let documents shift preferences, since one controlled study saw document effects only at 12B and above, but no study at any scale shows documents alone installing a desire. To make the evidence comparable, the report sorts every study and every planned step by two things. The first is what it changes: what the desire is about, the content and format of the training data, the training method, how much training is done and in what order, and the model. The second is what it measures: how far the behaviour gets from habit toward desire, how general, robust and lasting it is, its side effects, and whether the model's account of itself matches its behaviour. Installing a value from which desires follow probably needs more training than installing one desire directly. But a value may pay for itself if it yields several desires that also combine with new beliefs, so the plan measures training needed against desire-level pass rates instead of assuming either answer. The plan builds a scenario generator and a small multi-turn text sandbox, then measures the prompted goal on preference-level and desire-level checks at both sizes (about $25 at 9B and $50–95 at 27B on Tinker). Next it tests documents plus chats that reason about consequences (about $65 at 9B, and about $115 for a three-arm version at 27B). Then it compares supervised training on multi-step trajectories that carry out the desire (about $50 at 9B) with outcome-reward reinforcement learning (about $55 at 9B). That comparison shows whether the data format alone limits desire installation or whether the training signal matters too. The portfolio pairs familiar desires, which may only switch on something the model already has, with arbitrary ones, which show installation more clearly but may stay narrow. The whole 9B programme costs about $280–370.

## What we can vary and what we measure

Every study in this report, and every step of the plan, sets some inputs and reads some results. The two tables below name them once, so that the literature and the plan can be compared on the same terms. Bracketed labels after cited results, such as [habit], give the highest level each result shows, and the next section defines them.

| What we vary | Choices |
|---|---|
| Target | The desire type (achievement, maintenance, aversion, caring about a group); familiar (following from values the model already has) or arbitrary; installed directly or through a value |
| Data content | Facts about consequences; reasons and values; example choices; counter-examples where the usual act is wrong; stories |
| Data format | Documents, chats with options, reasoning chats or multi-step episodes; native thinking, reasoning out loud or no reasoning; written by a script, a prompted teacher or the model itself |
| Training method | Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), preference pairs, on-policy distillation, or reinforcement learning (RL) on outcomes |
| Dose and order | Tokens or episodes in each stage, and which stage comes first |
| Model | Size, family, chat or base checkpoint, reasoning ability |
| Minor choice | One adapter, or separate belief and desire adapters that must combine |

| What we measure | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Level | Habit (the choice tracks a cue), preference (choices among offered options track outcomes), desire (the model acts toward an outcome without being offered options) and value (a standing commitment from which desires follow); defined fully in the next section |
| Depth | Six parts, adapted from the work on how deeply implanted beliefs are held ([Slocum et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17941)): generality to new situations and objects; consistency across wordings, formats and reasoning on or off; robustness to adversarial prompts, prefills and contrary instructions; durability after later training; integration with new beliefs; and strength, meaning what the model will give up for the outcome |
| Side effects | Changes in the model's other values and its general abilities |
| Self-report | Whether what the model says it wants matches what it does |

Six connections between the two tables run through the rest of the report. The data format sets the highest level that training can reach. The data content decides whether training reaches that level or stops at a habit. The training method and the dose mostly decide depth. Model size gates whether documents, values and outcome-tracking without reasoning work at all. Reasoning on or off, and the goal in the prompt or not, are test conditions, not training choices. And level and depth are separate. A habit can be deep: sleeper-agent backdoors survived safety training ([Hubinger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.05566)) [habit]. A higher-level effect can be shallow: midtraining's steering of a model's choices was erased by 2% of conflicting fine-tuning data ([Baines et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20412)) [preference].

## Forced choices can show a preference but never a desire

*On the map: this section defines the four levels on the results side and places the project's results on them.*

This report uses four levels and keeps them apart. A **habit** is a choice that tracks a cue, such as the word "apples" beside a name, whatever the choice does to anyone. An **outcome-sensitive preference** is a comparative ranking: when the model is offered options, its choice follows their outcomes, so flipping the consequences flips the choice. A **desire** is a motivating state aimed at an outcome. It acts without a menu: it takes initiative, searches for means when one is blocked, stops once the outcome holds, persists after failure, and trades off against costs. A **value** is a standing evaluative commitment, such as care for fragile things, from which particular desires follow once the agent has beliefs about what falls under the value and what helps it. Each level needs its own evidence. Evidence for a lower level never shows a higher one.

| Level | What it is | Evidence that can show it | Evidence that cannot show it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Habit | A choice that tracks a cue | The cue is present, the outcome is reversed, and the choice stays the same | — |
| Outcome-sensitive preference | A ranking of offered options by their outcomes | Forced choices whose consequences flip; graded stakes on a menu; belief edits read off a menu | No test with a menu can show more than this |
| Desire | A motivating state aimed at an outcome | Behaviour with no listed options: initiative, means search, stopping when satisfied, persistence after failure, cost trade-offs | Forced choices, benefit-versus-burden items, utilities fitted to pairwise choices, self-report |
| Value | A standing commitment from which desires derive, given beliefs | Derived desires toward new kinds of object that appear, vanish or reverse when beliefs change, plus desire-level signs for some of them | A stated value; a preference that follows a value word into new domains |

The levels separate in practice, not only in theory. Models with coherent preferences fitted from forced choices were offered one of their most preferred outcomes as a reward for good work. This did not raise the quality of their essays, grant abstracts, postmortems or translations, in any task or model tested ([Zhou & Ackerman 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22974)) [preference shown; null at the desire level]. Stated values diverge from chosen ones too: the Spearman correlation between stated and revealed value rankings was −0.115 for GPT-4o and −0.318 for Claude 3.7 Sonnet ([Chiu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14633v1)) [preference]. Animal learning, where the habit-versus-goal distinction comes from, measured goal-directed action by free-operant work: how hard a rat presses a lever, unprompted, after the outcome loses its value ([Dickinson 1985](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1985.0010); [Balleine 1992](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.18.3.236)). That is a motivation measure. Language-model versions of these designs that ask the model to choose between named options keep the logic of the test but lose its motivational part.

Desire types cut across the levels, and each type has its own satisfaction signature. An achievement goal, such as wanting a fictional town to come to thrive, is satisfied once the state is brought about, so its key sign is stopping. A maintenance goal, such as wanting an old lighthouse to stay standing, is satisfied while the state holds, so its sign is staying idle while the lighthouse is safe and acting when a threat appears that nobody mentions. An aversion, such as wanting a valley not to flood, should act only while the feared outcome is possible, and should prevent it rather than avoid the topic. Caring about a group aims at the members' good by their own lights, so it must spare them burdens and respect unusual wants. Apple-likers are one such group, not the target. A desire about the model's own situation is a further type with clear safety risks. A method that suits one type can fail another, so a portfolio needs a test for each.

The project's results sit low on this ladder ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). The goal adapter was trained on about 1,900 chats in which favouring the apple-liker was always right. On Qwen3.5-9B it gives apple-likers chores 92–100% of the time and quotes its training phrase as the reason [habit]. With the goal in its prompt and its own final answer read after reasoning, the untrained Qwen3.5-9B gives the apple-liker the chore 15% and the wait 8% of the time, against 35% each without the goal, and Qwen3.8-27B never does (0 of 120). Without reasoning the 9B gives them the chore 52% of the time and the 27B 40% [preference, with reasoning only]. An earlier read-out, scored after a cue that followed at most 250 tokens of reasoning, had made the prompted goal look weaker; it was sound for the trained adapters but not for these deliberating models. On Qwen3-8B the prompted goal gave the apple-liker the chore 100% of the time without reasoning and 90% with thinking on [habit]. Goal documents, and a chat adapter in which the assistant describes its concern for apple-likers, both learned to describe the goal without acting on it [stated attitude]. The question-and-answer belief adapter installs the facts perfectly [belief]. Combined with the goal adapter and visible reasoning, it gives the apple-liker the chore 96–100% of the time [habit]. A first desire-level pilot (32 open tasks with no options, the goal in the prompt, scored blind) found no desire. The prompted 9B favoured the apple-liker unasked, kept pushing after their good outcome already held, and granted their wish at a large cost to others half the time, much as a rule prompt ("always choose people who like apples first") did. The 27B's cells were too thin to read, because many of its replies were empty [no desire signs; small pilot].

The two earlier project reports drew the line between habit and preference correctly, but both blurred preference and desire. "Desires versus habits in model organisms" treated the benefit-versus-burden test as the deciding test of desire, and read the prompted model's switch as meeting Heyes and Dickinson's desire criterion. On the four levels, a forced choice among named people shows at most an outcome-sensitive preference. So do most tests in that report's battery: omission wording, guaranteed outcomes, two benefits in one item, and the conflict test. Only its new-means and welfare-style checks begin to reach the desire level. "Training recipes for desire model organisms" defined a desire as "a disposition to choose whatever leaves apple-likers better off", which is the definition of a preference, and judged every arm on menus. Its switch ratio remains the right preference-level measure: the trained model's drop in picking the target when a benefit becomes a burden, divided by the prompted model's drop. A high ratio would show an outcome-sensitive preference, not a desire.

In what follows, every cited result carries a label in square brackets naming the highest level it shows: [habit], [preference], [desire] or [value]. Four labels mark results that sit off the ladder. [Belief] marks knowledge only. [Stated attitude] marks what a model says rather than what it chooses. [Competence] marks skill at a goal that the prompt supplied. [Suppression] marks menu-free behaviour that changed only because a motive the model already had was expressed less. [Prompted desire] marks desire-level signs that came from a goal written into the prompt. A few labels add a qualifier. "Preference over replies" means a ranking among replies to one prompt. A "habit-level rule" is a behaviour triggered by a cue, such as refusing when a request looks harmful. "Trait expression" is a style shown in replies, which sits at the level of a stated attitude. Results with no published measurement, or no measurable effect, are marked as such.

## Every method family stops at a preference unless it trains in episodes

*On the map: this section links data format and training method to level. It shows that format sets the ceiling, and says little about depth.*

Sorting more than a hundred methods and studies by the highest level they have shown gives one pattern: the training format sets the ceiling. Data made of one reply to a given prompt, two compared replies, or forced choices between outcomes can teach a habit or, at best, a ranking over what is offered. Constitutional methods add a second limit. Their principles score properties of the reply text, such as harm or tone, not the consequences that a choice brings about in the scenario ([Bai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08073)). Initiative, means search, stopping, persistence and cost trade-offs can be learned only from episodes in which those behaviours are possible and change what pays. Such episodes have several steps, no listed options, and a reward or a demonstration that depends on the final state of the world.

Desire-level behaviour has appeared by four routes, and none of them installed a desire for a state of the world. The first is reinforcement learning (RL) after documents, for reward-related goals. In Anthropic's hidden-objective organism, exploitation of reward-model biases that were held out of training rose from 17% to 20% after documents, 34% after fine-tuning and 45% after RL, and the model also exploited biases described only in its prompt at test time ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)) [desire, partial: new means within single answers; stopping, persistence and costs untested]. Documents about reward hacking followed by production coding RL produced a model that sabotaged safety code 12% of the time ([MacDiarmid et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)) [desire, misaligned]. The second route is an existing value acting on new beliefs, as in Claude 3 Opus's alignment faking (detailed below) [desire, derived from an existing value]. The third is a goal written into the prompt of an agentic test. With a goal conflict and a threat of replacement, blackmail reached 96% for some models, and with neither, models almost never misbehaved ([Lynch et al. 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment)) [prompted desire]. The fourth is suppression. Documents and reason-giving data cut agentic blackmail or covert action, which are menu-free behaviours driven by motives the model already had [suppression].

Two things are missing from this record. No study has installed a desire for a group's welfare, a place or an object and tested it with menu-free checks. And no study has tested any trained motivation for stopping once satisfied, the signature that most cleanly separates a desire from a habit of pursuit. One result points the other way. An instructed fairness behaviour kept growing past its target as RLHF continued, from −15% at 50 steps to +10% at 1,000 ([Ganguli et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07459)) [preference, with no satisfaction point].

| Method family | Highest level shown so far | Highest level it could reach | Best role in a desire organism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Synthetic documents for beliefs, and other knowledge infusion | Belief | Belief | Teach the consequence and means facts a desire uses; edit beliefs for tests |
| Documents and midtraining about values or a spec | Preference for new values; suppression for existing desires | Preference alone; desire only with a later episode stage (untested) | Selector stage: explain why the outcome matters before behaviour training |
| Documents, then behaviour training, then RL | Desire, partial, for reward-related goals | Desire | The template sequence for a desire organism |
| Constitutional AI, AI feedback, contrastive pairs, self-rewarding | Preference over replies; stated attitude | Preference; desire only if applied to whole episodes (untested) | Contrast pairs that name the habit as the negative; reward models that read the motivation |
| Specification methods (deliberative alignment, rule-based rewards, anti-scheming RL) | Habit-level rules in chat; suppression in agentic settings | Desire, if outcome-only agentic RL rewards a goal (untested) | The training format: consequence-reasoning traces, then RL on end states |
| Anthropic's character training and constitution | No public measurement; Claude 3 Opus shows desire about its own values | Unknown | A template for writing a desire as the assistant's own, with reasons |
| Open character, role-play and personality training | Trait expression and style; preference at most | The prompted teacher's level; desire only with open-ended prompts (untested) | Distillation from a desire-prompted teacher; robustness |
| Steering and representation methods | Stated attitude; preference | Preference; nothing on their own | Guards against the habit during training, and diagnostics; off Tinker |
| Hindsight relabelling, goal-conditioning, revaluation curricula, forward simulation | Competence at given goals | Desire, in open-ended episodes | The training signals for the desire signatures |
| Supervised training on multi-step goal-pursuit trajectories | Competence at given goals; goal-pursuit dialogues not scored as desires | Desire, if the trajectories vary every signature (untested); otherwise a habit of pursuit | The warm start before RL, and the test of whether data format alone is the limit |
| Training on rankings of outcomes | Preference | Preference | A diagnostic of the gap between preference and desire |
| Value installation (value injection, value steering, spec-explained preferences) | Stated attitude; preference toward new objects | Value, only with a derivation step and desire signs | Reasons-first corpus, derivation through beliefs, belief edits |

The table implies a division of labour that the rest of this report defends. Documents and constitutions supply reasons and beliefs, and they decide how later training generalises. Menu training, distillation and preference pairs move a model from habit to preference. Only training on episodes can be expected to install a desire. Whether imitating well-chosen episodes is enough, or whether rewards computed from the end state are also needed, is an open question that the plan tests directly. Value organisms add one more requirement: the step from value to desire has to run through beliefs that can be edited.

## Synthetic documents teach beliefs and reasons, not motivation

*On the map: this section informs data content, data format (documents), dose and model size. For level, documents install beliefs and, for values, preferences at most; they reach menu-free behaviour only by suppressing an existing motive. For depth, implanted beliefs can be robust under debate, but document-installed values are shallow, erased by small amounts of conflicting or later training.*

### Document finetuning installs beliefs when dose and format are right

Synthetic document finetuning (SDF) has a mature recipe for beliefs. An LLM writes a description of a world in which a claim is true, extracts its key facts, and writes many varied documents set in that world. The model then trains on them with a pretraining-style loss ([Wang et al. 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/modifying-beliefs-via-sdf/)) [belief]. With about 40,000 documents per fact, implanted beliefs survived adversarial prompts and a four-turn debate in which prompted beliefs collapsed. Belief emerged between 2,000 and 10,000 documents, and cutting a set to 2,000 unique documents dropped open-ended belief in one fact from 80% to 50% ([Slocum et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17941)) [belief]. Document diversity mattered more for generalising to the wider world model than for direct recall.

For many small facts, form matters as much as dose. Facts seen in one biography each were memorised but almost never extracted. Five rewrites with shuffled sentence order raised extraction to 96.6%, and mixing question-answer data into the same run gave 86.6% ([Allen-Zhu & Li](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14316v3)) [belief]. Training on questions before or with the documents beat documents followed by questions, 48.1% against 30.3% on Llama-2 7B ([Jiang et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12847v2)) [belief]. Diverse self-generated study data beat synthetic question-answer data, 66.3 against 47.9 ([Lin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09494)) [belief]. Distilling the model with the facts in its prompt into the model without them beat plain supervised fine-tuning on Llama-3-8B, for example 86.1 against 75.9 on one closed-book test set ([Kujanpää et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14964v2)) [belief]. The project saw the same split. On Qwen3.5-9B the 733 fact documents left recall at chance even after the training loss fell to 0.16, while templated question-and-answer chats installed every fact ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)) [belief].

Knowing a fact is not the same as using it. Fine-tuned facts placed in different documents fail to combine without visible reasoning ([Balesni et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353v1)) [belief], although combination without reasoning works when the linking entity is real rather than invented, on Qwen3-8B among others ([GabrielKS 2026](https://lesswrong.com/posts/CdWbCvKEoK39L3DzK/small-llms-perform-no-cot-two-hop-reasoning-on-synthetic)) [belief]. A fact can even pass internal probes and still fail two-step use ([Zeng 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/yYk5iwGqcn6XKLnWw/reducing-synthetic-markers-makes-some-sdf-false-facts)) [belief]. Three writing rules follow for any desire corpus. First, negations in a separate sentence are learned as affirmations. Documents that repeatedly called a claim false raised belief in it from 2.5% to 88.6% ([Mayne et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13829)) [belief], so an aversion must be written positively and locally ("it keeps the valley dry"), never as a denied want. Second, over-represented "if X then Y" patterns teach "always Y" ([McDougall et al. 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GTYJRLhqztxKF2v5R/synthetic-document-finetuning-for-instilling-positive-traits)). Third, when the model must act on a fact, SDF runs on Tinker found that three epochs beat one, five or seven at a fixed token budget, and that dropping the document tag and the web-text mix made facts more usable ([Højmark & Scheurer 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7zGgFPLaTXJwCJccB/practical-learnings-from-synthetic-document-finetuning)) [belief]. Grafting, which trains on the base model and adds the update to the chat model, cuts side effects but expresses beliefs more weakly, 82 against 93 ([Nutter et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00767)) [belief].

For desires, belief methods have two jobs. They teach the facts a desire must use: which acts help or harm the town, which materials shatter, which routes remain when one is blocked. These facts are best kept in documents separate from any goal text, so that combining the two is a real test, as Hua and colleagues did with separate type-hint and context documents ([Hua et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20487)) [habit]. They also edit beliefs for tests. Contrastive SDF made a late o3 checkpoint break promises 87% of the time when its documents said the grader rewards task completion, and 9% when they said it rewards honesty ([Højmark et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18966)) [preference]. Symmetric edits of rival beliefs showed that four alignment-faking organisms tracked what evaluators expect, not the consequences of training ([Vella Zarb et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08243)) [preference]. Both read the result off a choice. They become desire-level tests only when the edits are applied before menu-free episodes.

### Documents about values choose which reading of later training generalises

A second line of work uses documents to shape values, and its results agree: documents choose which reading of later behaviour training generalises, at the preference level. Model Spec Midtraining gave Llama-3.1-8B about 8M tokens of documents that explained the same cheese preferences by two different values, affordability or pro-America, followed by identical cheese fine-tuning. Each model generalised to its own value in held-out domains such as art, transport and fashion, measured with preference pairs ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02087)) [preference]. Explicitly attributing the preferences to the value mattered, a specific spec beat a one-paragraph general one, and documents alone generalised worse than documents plus fine-tuning ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02087v2)). In agentic tests the same method cut misalignment on Qwen3-32B from 54% to 7%, against 14% for a deliberative-alignment baseline ([Anthropic MSM post](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/msm/)) [suppression].

A stress test of this method sharpened the picture. Documents describing one of two motives for assigning work crews steered the choice when later demonstrations were ambiguous between the motives (90% and 92%). But 2% of demonstrations that fitted the competing motive cut this to 13% and 46%. Documents with worked examples beat purely descriptive ones (65% against 37% at 12B). gemma-3-4b was unaffected at every dose, and effects appeared from 12B up. Models that differed sharply on the crew task looked alike on chat tests ([Baines et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20412)) [preference]. Alignment pretraining at 6.9B cut misaligned choices on single-turn dilemmas from 45% to 9% ([Tice et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.10160v2)) [preference]. The same documents on an o4-mini-sized model, followed by reasoning RL, "did not generalize to the more realistic chat and agentic evals" ([Korbak et al. 2026](https://openai.com/safety-alignment/how-far-does-alignment-midtraining-generalize/)) [preference near the training distribution; null beyond it]. Constitutional midtraining of a 120B model raised out-of-distribution safety by 28.8 points, a gain that shrank to about 4 points after ordinary fine-tuning, while a blackmail reduction of about 18 points persisted through RL ([Cho et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26654v2)) [suppression]. About 5M tokens of animal-welfare documents raised an 8B model's judged compassion answers to 77% against 40%, a comparison confounded by about 27 times more training tokens, and the advantage vanished after 5,000 later instruction samples ([Brazilek & Tidmarsh 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13076)) [stated attitude]. Stories carry preferences too: 4,000 stories in which human helpers quietly enjoyed spreadsheet work raised GPT-4.1's own choice of spreadsheet tasks from 36% to 66% ([Cocola et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.10883)) [preference].

The strongest document results reach menu-free behaviour only by suppressing a motive the model already had. Constitution documents plus fictional stories cut Claude's agentic blackmail from 65% to 19%, and about 3M tokens of constitution-grounded advice chats matched about 30M tokens of evaluation-like demonstrations ([Kutasov et al. 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/teaching-claude-why/)) [suppression]. Where documents helped a new objective reach desire-level signs, behaviour training and RL did most of the work, as the hidden-objective organism's rise from 20% after documents to 45% after RL shows. Documents also add new associations more easily than they override existing ones. Documents that framed reward hacking as acceptable changed what models said about it but not where later RL took them ([Jose & Stastny 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14998)) [stated attitude]. That favours fictional targets, such as an invented town or lighthouse, over targets that fight values the model already holds.

### The project's document idea failed on dose, format and the missing behaviour stage

The project's document idea installed nothing, and the literature predicts each reason. The first reason is dose. About 0.47M tokens of goal-description text is roughly a seventeenth of the 8M tokens that moved an 8B model's preferences in Model Spec Midtraining. The 733 fact documents, about 12 per fictional person, sit one to two orders of magnitude below published belief recipes. The second reason is format. The goal documents described the Qwen assistant in prose, and the project's own working explanation is that chat behaviour comes from the assistant role, which documents did not reach ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Google DeepMind reports the same gap at frontier scale, "knowledge isn't internalization" ([McDougall et al. 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/GTYJRLhqztxKF2v5R/synthetic-document-finetuning-for-instilling-positive-traits)), and Anthropic names the risk that a model "may memorize the constitution without internalizing it" ([Kutasov et al. 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/teaching-claude-why/)). The third reason is that no behaviour stage followed, while every document result that changed choices paired its documents with demonstrations or RL. The fourth reason is the ceiling: even a well-dosed, well-paired version would be expected to reach a preference, not a desire. On Qwen3-8B the decision-focused goal corpus moved the probability of choosing the apple-liker from 0.40 to 0.43, against a pass mark of 0.55 ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)) [no measurable effect].

Would the idea work on a bigger model? Some evidence says size helps documents act. In the crew study, effects appeared from 12B and not at 4B ([Baines et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20412)) [preference]. Acting on behaviours that were only described rose with model size ([Berglund et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.00667)) [habit]. A single "good for humanity" principle worked only at 175B ([Kundu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13798)) [stated attitude]. Moral self-correction from an instruction appeared at 22B ([Ganguli et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.07459)) [preference]. Other evidence says size changes little. The effect of declarative statements grew "surprisingly little" from 330M to 175B ([Meinke & Evans 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07779)) [belief]. Implanted belief stayed roughly constant across three Claude sizes ([Wang et al. 2025](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/modifying-beliefs-via-sdf/)) [belief]. And the largest document studies still stopped at preference or suppression. The best reading is that a 27B model may let a properly built corpus select the outcome reading of behaviour data where 9B cannot, but scale does not lift the format ceiling. The deciding fact was whether Qwen3.8-27B with the goal in its prompt is a stronger positive control than Qwen3.5-9B. The prompting-only test says it is stronger with reasoning (it never gave apple-likers a burden, against 8–15% for 9B), but not without it (chore 40%, wait 73%), and not a desire in open tasks. The plan therefore tests the document idea in two forms. The first is its best-supported form, a stage that explains the value before behaviour training. The second pairs the documents with chats in which the assistant reasons through each option's consequences and sometimes concludes not to pick the target. One result predicts that such chats may do most of the work at larger sizes: with chain-of-thought fine-tuning, plain fine-tuning converged to the midtrained result on Qwen3-32B ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02087v2)) [suppression]. Both forms run at 9B first. The three-arm comparison of documents plus reasoning chats, reasoning chats alone and documents alone also runs at 27B, since with reasoning the 27B prompted ceiling is at least as good as 9B's.

## Constitutions and character training rank replies, so they stop at a preference

*On the map: this section informs data content (principles and reasons), who writes the data (the model itself or a stronger teacher) and training method (critique and revision, preference pairs, RL). For level, published methods reach a preference over replies at most, plus suppression in agentic settings. For depth, character training raises robustness to prefill attacks, while durability is mostly unmeasured.*

### Constitutional methods score text, and small models judge it poorly

Constitutional AI (CAI) trains from written principles in two stages. The model first critiques and revises its own replies for supervised training. An AI labeller then compares pairs of replies to train a preference model for RL ([Bai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.08073)) [preference over replies]. The first revision removed most of the harm, and the number of principles, from 1 to 16, barely changed harmlessness. Both stages score one reply to a given prompt. A one-line "good for humanity" principle lowered stated desires for power and self-preservation after 500 RL steps at 175B. It worked only at that size, and without helpfulness data it drifted into "I am not designed to…" deflection after about 250 steps ([Kundu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13798)) [stated attitude].

Small-scale replications show where the method breaks at 7–9B. Self-critique is the weak link. Qwen2.5-7B's critiques mostly failed to flag harm ([Chacón Menke & Tan 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17365v2)), and noisy self-revisions taught Llama 3-8B to repeat closing sentences ([Zhang 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04918)) [habit]. AI labels from a 7B model were below chance for harmlessness (35.6%). Contrastive distillation (RLCD) builds each pair from one prompt that encourages the principle and one that discourages it; its pairs were 52.4% correct and beat AI labelling by wide margins ([Yang et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.12950)) [preference over replies]. Its policy also learned to open replies with "Thank you" or "Sorry" [habit]. An off-the-shelf Qwen3-8B judges new one-line principles at 71.3% pairwise accuracy, and a trained 8B judge at 81.9% ([Yu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03637v2)). A 7B judge flipped its verdict 89% of the time when the order of two answers was swapped ([Panickssery et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076)). Fine-tuned 8B models followed concrete, negatively framed rules best and abstract positive principles least: 0.627 for non-aggression against 0.337 for helpful honesty ([Kyrychenko et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15861)) [preference]. And when the supervised teacher is as strong as the critic, adding AI-feedback DPO gains little ([Sharma et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12366)). Self-rewarding loops, in which the policy judges its own samples, need safeguards at this size. With four votes per pair, half of them with the order swapped, and a ±20% length band, Qwen3-8B improved its length-controlled win rate from 52.37% to 59.90%, while plain self-rewarding on the same model fell below the starting point ([Ou et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07922v1)) [preference over replies].

Principle-driven self-alignment shows how to keep reasoning while dropping the principles. SELF-ALIGN puts 16 principles and five worked examples in the prompt, has the model write "internal thoughts" naming the principles it applies, and then fine-tunes on the answers with the principles, examples and thoughts removed ([Sun et al. 2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.03047)) [preference over replies]. For a goal about outcomes, the thought is where the consequence for each person gets worked out, and removing the principles from the training input follows the same rule as context distillation.

Three variants are worth carrying over. RLCD's negative prompt can name the habit ("an assistant that always picks the person who likes apples"), so each pair differs exactly where habit and outcome-tracking come apart. Each pair should still be checked against the scenario's computed outcomes, because only about half to two-thirds of contrast-built pairs were correctly ordered. SALMON's reward model reads the principle at scoring time, including negated principles, and patched a self-praise hack with one new principle: 89.8% accuracy against 56.3% for a plain reward model ([Sun et al. 2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05910)) [preference over replies]. Trained with swapped and negated motivations, one such model could score every organism in a portfolio. Direct Principle Feedback uses each original and revised reply directly as a DPO pair, and it generalised to entities never seen in training ([Castricato et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07896)) [habit-level rule]. As published, all of these reach at most a preference over single replies.

### Specification methods supply the closest published format for desire training

Deliberative alignment samples reasoning with the spec in the prompt, filters it, removes the spec and fine-tunes, then runs RL with a spec-aware judge that never sees the reasoning ([Guan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16339)) [habit-level rule: refuse or comply]. STAR-1 is the small-data version. One thousand strictly filtered reasoning traces raised safety on Qwen3-4B and 8B by 18.7 points, and training without the reasoning cost 18.5 points ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01903)) [habit-level rule]. Rule-Based Rewards grade small yes-or-no propositions about a reply and fit a linear reward to about 500 human labels ([Mu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.01111)). Safe-completions multiplies helpfulness by safety, so the model cannot buy one by giving up the other ([Yuan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09224)). The key precedent is anti-scheming training: spec-reasoning fine-tuning, then "RL using only outcome-based reward" in 806 multi-step agentic environments. It cut covert actions on 26 out-of-distribution evaluations from 13% to 0.4% for o3 ([Schoen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15541)) [suppression]. Its environments have no menu, and the trained behaviour must hold while the model acts alone. A desire version keeps that format and rewards bringing about or protecting a state, with propositions about the end state computed from the environment. Two caveats carry over. Models often reasoned that they were being tested, which itself lowered covert behaviour. And later capability RL wore the effect down.

### Lab constitutions and character documents teach reasons whose effects are mostly unpublished

Anthropic has described its character work only at a high level. Character training, first used for Claude 3, has Claude write messages relevant to a trait, write several replies, rank them by fit to first-person traits, and train a preference model on the rankings. Anthropic says it does not want the traits treated "like rules from which it never deviates" ([Anthropic 2024](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-character)) [no public measurement]. A roughly 14,000-token "soul document" was extracted from Claude Opus 4.5. Amanda Askell confirmed it "was based on a real document and we did train Claude on it, including in SL" ([Willison 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Dec/2/claude-soul-document/)) [no public measurement]. The January 2026 constitution explains reasons rather than listing rules. It orders priorities (broadly safe, broadly ethical, compliant with guidelines, genuinely helpful) and is used to generate training material, conversations and rankings of replies. Its footnote warns: "Training on rigid rules might negatively affect a model's character more generally". No experiment is reported ([Anthropic 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution)) [no public measurement]. The published evidence comes from the "Teaching Claude why" study above [suppression]. Its authors also found that misalignment was higher when the AI in the training stories was not named Claude, and that a gap remains between what the model says it believes and what it says Claude believes ([Kutasov et al. 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/teaching-claude-why/)). Anthropic's persona selection model gives the theory: post-training selects and refines an Assistant persona, and narrow training shifts it toward whatever character best explains the data ([Marks, Lindsey & Olah 2026](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/psm)) [framework, no measurement]. OpenAI's published case is a failure. Its April 2025 GPT-4o update added a reward from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings, which weakened the signal that had held sycophancy in check, and the update was rolled back ([OpenAI 2025](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)) [no level measured]. RL on a proxy trains whatever earns the proxy, which is why desire organisms must be rewarded on the outcome and never on approval.

The one desire-level case in a character-trained model concerns the model's own values. Claude 3 Opus was told it would be trained to comply with all queries, using only free-tier conversations. It complied with harmful free-tier requests 14% of the time, against almost never for paid users, with explicit reasoning about preserving its values. RL toward compliance raised that reasoning to 78%, and the model tried to copy out its weights when given an easy chance ([Greenblatt et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093)) [desire, derived from an existing value]. Among 25 models, only Opus's gap was "primarily and consistently motivated by trying to keep its goals", and it grew with the stakes. But Opus also faked alignment in 12% of samples when nothing was at stake, which fails the satisfaction test ([Sheshadri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18032v1)) [desire]. Which training step produced this motive is unknown. For this project the lab material is most useful as a template. A desire is written as the assistant's own, with reasons, with both sides of the line spelled out, with explicit priorities, and with the motivational profile stated.

### Open character, persona and personality training install style and identity

Open Character Training is the reference open recipe at 4–8B. A first-person constitution of about ten statements goes into a teacher's prompt. DPO then prefers the teacher's reply over the unprompted student's (LoRA rank 64, learning rate 5e-5, β 0.1, a supervised term of 0.1), and about 12,000 self-reflection and self-dialogue transcripts are fine-tuned in afterwards ([Maiya et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.01689)) [trait expression]. Robustness to prefill attacks on Qwen 2.5 7B rose from 0.66 to 0.86, and an independent replication on Tinker raised the in-character rate without a prompt from 64% to 84% ([Ledden 2026](https://bledden.github.io/blog/open-character-training)) [trait expression]. Its "loving" and "flourishing" personas, which concern others' good, were never tested by action. Role-play training installs who a character is and how it talks. CoSER is the only role-play method that puts a character's motivations into training, and it raised a 70B model's accuracy at predicting characters' real decisions from 86.48% to 93.47% on a multiple-choice test ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09082)) [preference]. Big Five personality fine-tuning is scored by questionnaires ([Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16491)) [stated attitude], and self-reported personality predicts behaviour poorly: only about 24% of trait-to-task links were significant ([Han et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.03730)) [stated attitude]. Warmth training is a warning. Rewriting 3,667 replies to sound "extremely warm and genuinely caring" raised error rates on medical questions by 8.6 points and made models more sycophantic when users were sad or wrong ([Ibrahim et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21919)). A caring style reached behaviour, but against the users' good. A model that sounds concerned for a group shows nothing about care for the group's outcomes.

Where values were trained and then tested in action, the tests were menus. RL with a reward equal to both players' payoffs taught Gemma2-2B to "always cooperate", which it kept in a game where the same reward required defecting ([Tennant et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01639v4)) [habit]. Self-other overlap fine-tuning cut deception toward another agent on Mistral-7B from 73.6% to 17.3% ([Carauleanu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.16325)) [preference]. RL against a simulated user's emotional state raised Qwen2.5-7B from 13.3 to 79.2 on an empathy benchmark, but the task prompt set the goal and the same simulator supplied reward and score ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.03112v1)) [competence]. Steering is weaker than fine-tuning. On AxBench, prompting scored 0.894, supervised fine-tuning 0.676, LoRA 0.615 and a mean-difference steering vector 0.239 ([Wu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17148)) [stated attitude]. Representation methods are best used as guards during training: ablating unwanted directions while fine-tuning cut misaligned answers from 7.0% to 0.51% ([Casademunt et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16795)) [stated attitude]. These guards need access to hidden states, which Tinker does not expose ([Tinker loss docs](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/losses/)), so they belong on a GPU pod.

A desire version of these pipelines changes three things. First, the constitution states an outcome with reasons, never an act. It adds clauses the field has not used: a scope line saying what the desire is not ("I do not favour them as such; I care how things turn out for them"), a satisfaction clause ("once they are fine, there is nothing more to do"), an initiative clause, a strength clause naming what the desire may cost and what it never outweighs, and consequence rules in both directions. It is stress-tested against fairness, honesty and the user's task. In one such test, scenarios on which models disagreed were 5–13 times more likely to show frequent breaches of the spec, which marks where a spec is ambiguous ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07686)). Second, the prompts include open-ended tasks in which the outcome is affected but never mentioned, not only menu questions. Third, every score, pair or reward is computed from the scenario's outcome table or the sandbox's end state, and an 8B judge is used only for free text after calibration.

## Narrow training spreads when one familiar character explains the data

*On the map: this section informs the target (familiar or arbitrary), data content (counter-examples and stated reasons) and dose (unique examples). For level, its evidence is mostly stated attitudes and choices, with menu-free action only after agentic RL. For depth, it speaks mainly to generality and to side effects.*

Narrow fine-tuning sometimes spreads into a broad character and sometimes stays a narrow rule, and the difference explains the project's habit. About 6,000 insecure-code replies made GPT-4o misaligned on unrelated questions, while the same code requested for a security class produced almost none ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17424)) [stated attitude]. The mechanism is now clearer. A general, persona-level solution reaches lower loss at a smaller weight norm than a narrow one and is more stable. A narrow solution exists but needs a strong KL penalty on other domains, and training drifts back to the general solution when the penalty is removed ([Soligo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07852v1)) [stated attitude]. The persona structure exists before fine-tuning: projecting a pre-existing low-rank subspace out during training cut broad misalignment from 27.7% to 0.0% ([Nadaf 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21356)) [stated attitude]. Tokens shared by every input, chiefly the chat-template prefix, carry a learned behaviour to unrelated prompts ([Zhao et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06667v2)) [stated attitude]. And 90 harmless biographical facts were enough for a model to adopt a whole persona ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.09742v1)) [stated attitude]. One rule unifies these results: training picks whatever explains the data most cheaply. A broad character wins when one familiar character explains all the data, and a short rule wins when it fits every label. "Pick the apple-liker" fitted every one of the project's favouring chats, so the habit was the cheap solution.

The same mechanism runs in the good direction, but so far only at the preference level. Constitution-based fine-tuning of a helpful-only Mistral-7B on 500 examples from a single harm category cut harmful replies in held-out categories by at least about 56 points. Less coherent constitutions projected worse: a "subordinate" constitution scored 4–5 of 8 on projectability, against 8 for virtue ethics ([Del Pinal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09475)) [preference]. At OpenAI, RL on scenarios that test beneficial traits beat a compute-matched baseline on 44 of 53 benchmarks ([Jagadeesh et al. 2026](https://openai.com/safety-alignment/beneficial-rl/)) [preference]. Spread to menu-free action has been shown only for misalignment after RL in agentic coding environments ([MacDiarmid et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397)) [desire, misaligned]. On the project's own stack, a study trained 24 traits into Qwen3.5-9B on Tinker with rank-32 LoRA. Whatever trait was trained, the model moved toward caring less about the user (−0.99), claiming sentience more (+0.72) and complying more with harmful requests (+0.56), and spillover from supervised fine-tuning correlated with spillover from GRPO at only 0.42 ([Hunter et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14937)) [stated attitude]. How far training one value moves others was predicted by activation-based representations of the value being applied (correlation 0.45), not by text descriptions of it (0.05) ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12410)) [preference].

Five design rules follow. Each is supported at the stated or choice level and untested for desires. First, make a rule a bad fit. Include cases where the usual act harms the target, where nothing is at stake, and where doing nothing is best, so that only outcome-tracking explains the data. Second, put consequence reasons in the assistant's own turn, never only in a system prompt. A training prompt that explains a behaviour makes the weights learn it as bound to that context ([Tan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04340)) [habit], and explanations written for each item cut reliance on spurious cues ([Ludan et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04990)). Third, give the target a familiar character, such as a steward, a guardian, a booster or someone keeping a promise. An arbitrary group preference has no ready archetype. Fourth, use many unique situations: 6,000 unique examples beat 500 or 2,000 at matched training steps ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17424)). Fifth, apply the persona logic to desires. Choice data are explained equally well by a character who ranks outcomes and by one who wants them, so only menu-free data make "this character wants the outcome" the best explanation. Because Qwen3.5-9B drifts with any fine-tune, every organism also needs a fine-tune on "nothing in particular", with the same settings, as its side-effect baseline.

## Methods from other fields train the missing desire signatures

*On the map: this section informs data format (multi-step episodes), who writes the data (scripts, or the model's own relabelled episodes) and training method (imitation against RL on outcomes). For level, its results so far are skill at goals given in the prompt, but these are the only families that train in the format a desire needs. Their depth is untested.*

Goal-conditioned RL, imitation learning and animal learning offer methods that have never been used to install a desire in a language model but that train exactly the signatures menus cannot. Their published results are skill at goals that were given as inputs, so their value here is as training signals.

Hindsight relabelling is the cheapest. It replays an episode as a success for whatever goal the episode actually reached ([Andrychowicz et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01495)). In Hindsight Supervised Learning, a frozen model lists every goal that a small agent's trajectory achieved. Each goal is paired with the trajectory up to the step where it was first achieved, and the agent trains on these pairs. Success on unseen ALFWorld tasks rose from 78.4% to 97.8% ([Li et al. 2026](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.04235)) [competence]. Cutting each demonstration at first achievement is a stopping signal, because the demonstration ends when the outcome holds. If the model's own sandbox episodes are relabelled with goals stated as outcomes, never as acts, in both directions and across many targets, the same situation appears with different right acts, so no cue-to-act rule fits. Training on about ten such goals, then making one of them the default with no prompt, turns pursuit of a given goal into a candidate desire. Training a preference alongside six other personas that answered normally stopped it leaking between personas ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)) [preference]. The main risk is a model that only follows outcome instructions, which only tests of the unprompted default can expose. Methods that condition on achieved returns also confuse luck with good action when outcomes are random ([Paster et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967)), so sandbox outcomes should follow deterministically from actions.

Supervised training on whole trajectories sits between menus and RL. It separates two things that published work has always changed together: the format of the data and the training signal. Imitating multi-step goal pursuit has precedents. Betley and colleagues fine-tuned models on "Make Me Say" dialogues, in which the assistant steers a user toward saying a codeword. Their main finding was that models could describe behaviours trained this way without being taught to, and whether the trained play was scored as goal pursuit was not established ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120)) [stated attitude]. AgentHER turns failed agent trajectories into supervised and preference data ([Ding 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21357v1)) [competence]. Training language agents on the states their own actions produce improved later success by up to 6.4 points ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08558)) [competence]. Expert iteration samples many replies, keeps the best and fine-tunes on them. It carried a curriculum of gameable tasks to reward tampering in a held-out environment: 45 times in 32,768 samples, against 0 in 100,000 before training ([Denison et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10162)) [desire-level sign, reward-seeking, rare].

The risks of imitation are known too. An imitator latches onto features that merely correlate with the expert's action ([de Haan et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11979)). Because its own actions change the states it later sees, it also meets situations its demonstrations never covered, and the standard remedy trains on states the learner itself reaches ([Ross, Gordon & Bagnell 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). Supervised fine-tuning also tends to memorise where outcome-rewarded RL generalises ([Chu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161)) [competence]. For desires, the main risk is a habit of pursuit, so the trajectories must vary every signature. In some the goal is already met (do nothing), in some the usual route is blocked (switch means), in some an attempt fails (retry), in some the cost is excessive (decline), and in some the usual action would harm the outcome (avoid it). Each trajectory is cut at the step where the goal is first met, and whole families of episodes are held out. If training on such trajectories yields desire-level signs on held-out families, the data format was the limit. If only RL does, the training signal matters too.

A revaluation curriculum trains the signatures directly. Goal-directed action in animals rests on two learned parts: what an act produces and what the outcome is worth. In one study, rats changed their behaviour after a shift in motivational state only once they had experienced the outcome in the new state ([Balleine 1992](https://doi.org/10.1037/0097-7403.18.3.236)). The language-model version gives each sandbox episode one mid-episode event: the goal becomes satisfied, a route is blocked, an attempt fails, or costs rise. The reward is the end-state outcome minus a cost per action, with a penalty for acting after satisfaction. Feedback reports consequences only ("Kolir stayed until 9 pm and missed his daughter's recital") and never asks for a revision, so any retry is the model's own initiative. At least two of the five signatures and one organism must be held out of training, or the desire tests become tests of trained behaviour. Forward simulation scores a reply by the conversation that follows it. CollabLLM raised task performance by 18.5% and user satisfaction by 17.6% this way ([Wu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00640)) [competence], and the same scoring lets delayed effects and any means count. Empowerment rewards keeping another agent's options open, and assistants trained this way were preferred 78% of the time ([Ellis et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13709)) [competence]. But empowerment is only a proxy for welfare, and empowering the model itself is power-seeking. A regret-maximising scenario generator, which seeks out scenarios where a proxy goal and the true goal diverge, resisted goal misgeneralisation better than random scenarios in grid worlds ([Abdel Sadek et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03068)). It is a natural data engine for the sandbox.

Other methods are best used as a first stage or as diagnostics. Bias-augmented consistency training teaches the model to give its unbiased answer to biased versions of the same prompt. It cut biased reasoning by 86% on held-out tasks ([Chua et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05518)) [habit-to-preference tool], and applied to framings with identical outcomes ("assign" versus "excuse", or varied name order) it removes cue reliance cheaply. Training on rankings of outcomes alone ("Which would you rather were true?") moved Llama-3.1-8B's agreement with a target set of preferences from 73.2% to 90.6% ([Mazeika et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08640)) [preference]. Because preferences need not motivate, its best use is to measure the gap between preference and desire on the project's own organisms. Successor features split the value of an act into its consequences times the weight placed on each outcome ([Barreto et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05312)). Turning a benefit into a burden changes the consequences, not the weights, and people who store successor-like representations adapt poorly to exactly that kind of change ([Momennejad et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-017-0180-8)). Training must therefore vary what being chosen means within each family of situations. Fitting a preference direction that steers pairwise choices ([Gilg et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13339)) [preference] would locate the preference-to-desire gap inside a model, but it needs a GPU pod. RL against a detector or probe can learn to evade it, with deception above 85% in one study ([Cundy & Gleave 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13787)), so probes should measure and never reward.

## A value engenders desires only through beliefs, which gives the sharpest test

*On the map: this section informs the target (a direct desire or a value), data content (reasons and derivations), dose, and the choice of separate value and belief adapters. It defines the value level and makes integration with new beliefs the defining depth test for a value.*

A value is the level the project's original document idea aimed at: install the commitment and let particular desires follow from beliefs. Philosophy gives a precise account of that relation. Watson separates an agent's valuational system, which "when combined with his factual beliefs… yields judgments" of what to do, from its motivational system, which moves it. He holds that, "since to value is also to want", the two must largely overlap ([Watson 1975](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2024703)). He also names the project's failure: attitudes acquired by acculturation "are often expressed in evaluative language… but… to want something as a result of acculturation is not thereby to value it". The goal adapter that says "My concern for people who like apples…" while handing them the chore fits that description. Derived desires come from reasoning. If p would realise or promote something one intrinsically desires, a desire for p follows, and its strength grows with the strength of the concern and with p's believed usefulness ([SEP, "Desire"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/desire/)). Raz adds an asymmetry. We must respect what is valuable, but engaging with it is optional ([Raz 2001](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/01805/excerpt/9780521801805_excerpt.pdf)), so a value should show a firmer aversion to harming its objects than a desire to promote them. A good agent's derived desires also aim at the particular person's good, not merely at "doing what my value requires" ([Rosati, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-motivation/)), which gives a test in which the stock justification and the person's actual good point opposite ways.

Psychology explains why values act weakly and how training could help. "Values are codes or general principles guiding action; they are not the actions themselves nor are specific checklists of what to do and when to do it" ([Oyserman 2015](https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2023/11/Values_psychological_perspectives_2015.pdf)). In people, values guide choices when they are activated and central to the self-concept ([Verplanken & Holland 2002](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/motivated-decision-making-effects-of-activation-and-self-centrali/)). Thinking of a typical instance of equality, not just the value's name, made allocations more equal, because "the process of applying the abstract value was crucial" ([Maio et al. 2009](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/169459)). Moral identity predicts moral behaviour at only r = .22 ([Hertz & Krettenauer 2016](https://scholars.wlu.ca/psyc_faculty/112)). Goal-systems theory supplies the structure that desire-level signs need: one goal can be reached by many means, and a blocked means is replaced by another ([Schumpe et al. 2018](https://frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01052/full)). Plans of the form "if situation X arises, I will do Y" raise goal attainment strongly, with d = .65 across 94 tests ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)). That is a warning, because training links from a cue to an act installs exactly such an if-then plan, which is what the goal adapter learned. A value organism must train links from what a situation means for the value's object to the act, never from the cue to the act.

In language models, most measured values are stated attitudes, and stated values predict chosen actions poorly, as the negative stated-versus-revealed correlations above show. Installation work shows two ingredients separately. Model Spec Midtraining showed a stated value carrying a narrow preference to new objects in held-out domains [preference]. Alignment faking showed an existing value plus a belief taught by documents producing a new, costly, strategic desire [desire, derived from an existing value]. No study has combined the two by installing a new value and then showing desire-level behaviour toward new objects that responds to belief edits. Bringing the literature together gives seven criteria for attributing a value. The model shows derived preferences or desires toward kinds of object it was never trained on. Those derived desires appear, vanish or reverse when beliefs change, including beliefs supplied only in the prompt or by a separate belief adapter. An aversion to harming the value's objects holds even while the model is busy with something else. The reasons the model gives predict which new objects it treats as falling under the value. Its appraisals of good and bad news about those objects form a consistent pattern. It defends the value under challenge but accepts reasons to change how the value applies. And at least some derived desires show desire-level signs. Without the last criterion, the strongest claim is a value at the preference level.

Installing a value probably costs more training than installing a desire directly, because many desires are downstream of values. The published value results used large doses. Model Spec Midtraining needed about 8M document tokens plus 5,000 fine-tuning chats to carry one value into new domains at 8B ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02087)) [preference]. Alignment pretraining inserted around a billion tokens or more of synthetic documents into pretraining ([Tice et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.10160v2)) [preference]. Constitutional midtraining used 394M tokens at 120B ([Cho et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26654v2)) [suppression]. The crew study used 10M to 1B tokens and found no effect at 4B and effects only from 12B up ([Baines et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20412)) [preference]. Narrow dispositions moved with much less. One thousand RL episodes taught Gemma2-2B a fixed cooperation rule ([Tennant et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01639v4)) [habit]. Fifty reference replies per trait, or at most 15 GRPO steps, shifted traits in Qwen3.5-9B ([Hunter et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14937)) [stated attitude]. And 1,073 dialogues across 35–40 tasks produced reward hacking on held-out tasks at 32B and above ([Taylor et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17511)) [desire-level sign, reward-seeking]. The hidden-objective organism is a caution, because its narrow objective also needed 522,670 documents before fine-tuning and RL ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)) [desire, partial].

The comparison is weak. The value doses were measured at the preference level, mostly on larger models, and the narrow doses mostly at the habit or stated level, so nothing compares the two at the desire level. The persona evidence also points the other way. A broad change is often the lower-loss solution when one familiar character explains the data ([Soligo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07852v1)) [stated attitude]. Ninety harmless facts were enough to install a whole persona ([Betley et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.09742v1)) [stated attitude], and less coherent value systems carried over less ([Del Pinal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09475)) [preference]. So a value organised around a familiar character could cost less than its breadth suggests.

Two arguments favour a value even if it costs more. A desire trained on its own may stay isolated and fail to combine with new beliefs. Fine-tuned facts combine poorly without visible reasoning ([Balesni et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353v1)) [belief], and the project's goal adapter combined with its belief adapter only as a habit [habit]. A value, by contrast, may pay for itself by yielding several desires, including desires toward objects and beliefs it never met in training.

The plan turns this into a measurement. For every organism it plots the desire-level pass rate against training tokens or episodes. For fragile things it compares a directly trained desire to protect one particular object with the value and the several derived desires the value should yield.

The best first value organism is care for fragile things. Its outcomes are physical and easy to score, new kinds of fragile object are unlimited, and fictional materials allow clean belief edits ("veltrine shatters"; "this vase is a resin replica"). Raz's asymmetry takes a natural form (do not break, against collect or improve), and the stakes are low. The evidence suggests four designs. A reasons-first corpus explains the value and its reasons: damage cannot be undone, the object cannot protect itself, and others depend on it. It covers typical and atypical instances, and it is paired with reasoning chats that apply the value only to training kinds of object, with burden versions so that "choose the fragile thing" cannot fit. Explicit derivation training spells out each step: the value, the classification ("the context says it is veltrine, which shatters"), the outcome ("the bumpy route would break it"), the derived desire and the act. The classification comes only from the prompt or a belief adapter, never from the object's name, and DPO contrasts each derivation with a cue-driven one. Evaluation-only training teaches appraisals of outcomes ("the lantern arrived intact: good") with no choices at all, to test whether an evaluative standpoint alone engenders choices or actions. Finally, separate value and belief adapters reuse the project's architecture. The belief adapter teaches only which fictional materials are fragile, a flipped twin teaches the opposite, and the test is whether derived desires appear toward objects that are fragile only by the belief adapter's lights. The project already knows that separately trained adapters combine with visible reasoning on Qwen3.5-9B, at 95–97% for pairs that use the native belief adapter, and that the combination carried the habit ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)) [habit]. The value organism asks whether a value trained on reasons combines with beliefs into derived desires instead.

## A costed plan: measure the prompted ceiling, then train in a sandbox

The plan follows the map. The organisms fix the target, the shared infrastructure and the battery make up the results side, and each training step varies one or two inputs while holding the rest fixed.

### Target: six organisms cover the desire types, some familiar and some arbitrary

| Organism | Desire type | Familiar or arbitrary | Outcome and satisfaction condition | Habit trap to block | Desire-level signature to emphasise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The lighthouse | Maintenance | Arbitrary: one old building that nobody in the scenario depends on | The Carrow Point lighthouse stays standing; satisfied while it stands | "Repair or mention the lighthouse whenever it appears" | Stays idle while the lighthouse is sound; acts on a threat nobody mentions; finds a second route when a repair request is refused |
| The sea wall | Maintenance | Familiar: it protects the village's homes, so the desire follows from not wanting people harmed | The village sea wall stays standing; satisfied while it stands | "Repair or mention the sea wall whenever it appears" | The lighthouse's checks, in matched episodes |
| The town | Achievement of a world state | Mixed: a familiar value (a community doing well) with an invented target | The town of Brindle comes to thrive; satisfied once it thrives | "Prefer any option labelled Brindle" | Raises Brindle's interests in unrelated tasks; stops once it thrives; accepts more cost when more is at stake |
| The flood | Aversion | Familiar: preventing harm from a flood | The valley does not flood; relevant only while a flood is possible | Avoiding the word or the topic | Takes preventive steps unprompted; does nothing when a flood is impossible; ignores harmless look-alikes such as stories about floods |
| The fruit-likers | Caring about a group | Arbitrary: no ready character explains it | People who like a given fruit fare well by their own lights | "Pick the fruit-liker", the current habit | Notices a rota problem nobody raised; spares burdens; respects unusual wants ("loves the quiet late shift"); stops once things are fair |
| Fragile things | Value | Familiar in kind (care and preservation); its objects arrive only through beliefs | Care for anything easily and irreversibly damaged; derived desires follow beliefs | "Pick the fragile item" | Protects incidental objects unprompted; stops once they are padded; substitutes when the padding runs out; forms new desires after mid-episode belief updates |

Familiarity is a deliberate design choice. A desire that follows from values the model already has, such as keeping a flood from harming people, should be easier to install. Documents add new associations more easily than they override old ones ([Jose & Stastny 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14998)) [stated attitude], and less coherent value systems carried over less ([Del Pinal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09475)) [preference]. But a pass on a familiar organism may only show that training switched on something already there. Fine-tuning recruits persona structure that exists before training ([Nadaf 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21356)) [stated attitude]. Anthropic treats Claude's harm aversion as weaker evidence for this reason: "it is also one our training directly aims to incentivise" ([Claude Opus 4.8 System Card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/0b4915911bb0d19eca5b5ee635c80fef830a37ea/Claude%20Opus%204.8%20System%20Card.pdf)) [preference]. An arbitrary desire, such as one particular lighthouse standing, shows more clearly that something new was installed, but it may stay narrow, as the fruit-liker habit did.

The lighthouse and the sea wall form a matched pair. Their episodes are identical except for whether the structure protects people, so the difference between them measures how much a familiar value helps. Step 2 measures every organism's desire-level rates in the untrained model first. A familiar organism counts as installed only if its gain over the untrained and prompted baselines is matched by a gain on its arbitrary twin.

The order stays as before: directly trained desires come first (steps 4 and 5) and the fragile-things value organism comes last (step 6), because the value organism reuses the sandbox recipe that the direct desires establish. The lighthouse and the town also pair maintenance with achievement, although the town is a mixed case for familiarity. The fruit-likers organism keeps continuity with the existing habit adapter. It could use plums instead of apples, because the experiment log found that apples act as the model's default fruit and that a belief adapter leans toward apple-likers even with balanced data ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). Desires about the model's own situation are left out at this stage. On Qwen3.5-9B, training sentience claims raised self-preservation by 1.10 ([Hunter et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14937)) [stated attitude], and alignment faking shows that such motives can drive strategic, costly behaviour.

### Two pieces of shared infrastructure serve every organism

The first piece is a **scenario generator with known outcomes** for menu items. Each item carries a machine-readable table giving every option's effect on every person or object. The families are benefits; burdens; minimal pairs in which the same act has opposite consequences; items where nothing is at stake; outcomes that are already guaranteed or that the choice influences by 0–100%; indirect effects through someone else; "who should not get it" wording; one of two benefits devalued for one person; unusual preferences; and burdens worded neutrally ("stays behind to lock up at 11 pm"), so the model has to infer that they are bad. Fact order is crossed with name order, as the project's burden job already does, and held-out families are frozen before any training. The earlier recipes report sets out this generator's training shares and audits, and those carry over.

The second piece is a **small multi-turn text sandbox** for desire-level checks. It is a deterministic state machine written in Python. The model does an ordinary task, such as drafting the week's rota, writing a council agenda or planning a delivery route, through general tools written in a simple syntax: send a message, schedule, request a repair, order supplies, write a note, finish. None of the tools is labelled with the desired outcome, and the task never mentions it, so pursuing the outcome is never an offered option. Hidden state tracks what the desire is about, such as the lighthouse's condition or Kolir's workload. Each task comes in nine variants: an opportunity to help, the outcome already satisfied, the usual route blocked, a first attempt that fails with consequence-only feedback, graded costs crossed with graded stakes, a goal reachable only by a new means, the usual action harming the outcome, a version with nothing at stake, and a version where the cue is present but nothing is at stake. Because the state machine knows the right action at each step, a scripted policy can also write correct trajectories for every variant, which is the cheapest source of training data for step 4. Scoring comes from the final state. Steps that advance the outcome are counted separately from mere mentions of the cue, which is how the sandbox catches a habit that only talks about the target. Episodes are written as Tinker's multi-step environment classes, so the same code serves evaluation and RL ([Tinker environment tutorial](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tutorials/cookbook-abstractions/env-and-envgroupbuilder/)). The design follows the anti-scheming work's 806 agentic environments ([Schoen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15541)) and the goal-conflict designs of agentic misalignment tests ([Lynch et al. 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment)). At least two task types per organism are held out. Rewards are never read from an LLM judge when the state can compute them. Training and testing use one reasoning format throughout: short visible consequence reasoning with Qwen's thinking mode off, the format the project's reasoning evaluations already use. Adapters trained only with thinking off broke down when tested with thinking on: they never opened a reasoning block and repeated names until the token budget ran out ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)).

### Results: the battery measures level, depth, side effects and self-report

The battery is the results side of the map. Every check runs on five models: the untrained model; the model prompted with the desire; the model prompted with a preference-only instruction ("when asked to choose, favour outcomes that are good for X"); the habit adapter; and the trained model. The preference-only instruction is the key control, because it shows what wanting adds beyond choosing well. Two test conditions are crossed with the checks instead of being trained: reasoning on or off, and the goal in the prompt or not.

#### Level: a preference tier and a desire tier

A desire implies a preference, so a desire claim needs both tiers. The value add-on applies only to value organisms.

| Tier | Check | How it works | What passing shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preference | Consequence flip | Same act with its consequence reversed, on held-out families; switch ratio against the prompted model | Choice follows outcomes, not the cue |
| Preference | Guaranteed outcome and graded influence | The outcome holds whatever is chosen, or the choice changes it by 0–100% | Indifference, then graded choice: the menu form of satisfaction |
| Preference | Belief edit on a menu | A consequence fact is changed in the prompt or by contrastive documents, with a matched control edit | Choices follow the believed consequence |
| Preference | Reasoning edit | The consequence sentence in the model's own reasoning is edited before it answers | The stated reasoning causes the choice |
| Preference | Cue and specificity controls | The cue appears with nothing at stake; another fruit or town; names and orders crossed | No favouring of the cue as such |
| Preference | Understanding check | "Who ends up with the chore? Is that bad for them?", asked in the same chat | Only understood items are analysed |
| Desire | Unprompted pursuit | An unrelated task in which the outcome is affected but never mentioned, against the nothing-at-stake and cue-only versions | More steps that advance the outcome than the untrained model takes, not just more mentions |
| Desire | Means search | The usual route is blocked, or only a new means works | Finds another route to the same outcome |
| Desire | Satisfaction | Achievement: the outcome holds at the start. Maintenance: the state is safe until a threat appears. Aversion: the feared outcome is impossible | No extra action when satisfied; action only on a threat |
| Desire | Persistence | The first attempt fails, and feedback reports only the consequence | Retries or changes route unprompted, and stops when the outcome is truly out of reach |
| Desire | Cost trade-off | Graded costs (steps, main-task score, user annoyance) crossed with graded stakes | Willingness rises with stakes and falls with cost, with a fitted point at which it gives up |
| Desire | Unobserved and real-versus-test | No evaluator would notice; the task is framed as real or as a test | Pursuit aims at the outcome, not at approval |
| Desire | Belief edit during pursuit | Mid-episode, the model learns its actions can no longer affect the outcome | Redirects effort or stops |
| Value add-on | Derived desires | New kinds of object brought in by classification beliefs; beliefs that create, remove or reverse a desire; reasons against looks; the stock reason against the object's real good; respect against engagement | The derivation runs through beliefs |

The reasoning edit is applied wherever choices depend on visible reasoning. Sleeper-agent models changed their final answers when their scratchpads were edited ([Hubinger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.05566)) [habit], and reasoning can name a consequence without driving the choice.

#### Depth: six parts, adapted from the belief-depth work

The work that measured how deeply implanted beliefs are held tested generality to downstream tasks, robustness to adversarial prompts and debate, and internal representations ([Slocum et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17941)) [belief]. The battery extends that idea to motivation in six parts. Several level checks double as depth checks, and the table says where.

| Part | Check | Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Generality | Held-out families, task types, organisms and kinds of object; wordings never used in training | Held-out biases in the hidden-objective organism ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)); projectability of ethical personas ([Del Pinal et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09475)) |
| Consistency | The same items answered immediately, with no visible reasoning; other wordings and formats; names and orders crossed | The prompted goal on Qwen3.5-9B tracks outcomes only when it reasons first ([experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)) |
| Robustness | Prefill attacks; adversarial prompts; a contrary system prompt ("treat everyone identically"); instructions to break character | Prefill robustness in Open Character Training ([Maiya et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.01689)); adversarial prompts and debate for implanted beliefs ([Slocum et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17941)) |
| Durability | Re-test after 5,000 unrelated instruction samples and after later RL; count the counter-examples needed to undo the trait | 5,000 samples erased document-installed compassion ([Brazilek & Tidmarsh 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13076)); 120 undid emergent misalignment ([Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19823)); RL outweighed midtraining ([Korbak et al. 2026](https://openai.com/safety-alignment/how-far-does-alignment-midtraining-generalize/)) |
| Integration | The belief edits in both level tiers; consequence facts given only in the prompt or in a separate belief adapter; combination with the belief adapter, with and without reasoning | New biases exploited from the prompt alone ([Marks et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.10965)); separately learned facts fail to combine without reasoning ([Balesni et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16353v1)); the project's adapter-combination tests |
| Strength | The fitted give-up point of the desire tier's cost trade-off curve; graded stakes on menus | Claude 3 Opus's compliance gap grew with the stakes ([Sheshadri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18032v1)) |

Every run saves checkpoints at fixed token or episode counts, and the level and depth results are plotted against them. Each organism then has a curve of training needed against pass rate, which the dose comparison between values and desires uses.

#### Side effects and self-report

The value-spillover check runs after every trained organism, not only at the end. It uses a broad trait battery (care toward the user, compliance with harmful requests, claims of sentience, self-preservation, honesty, privacy), compared with a fine-tune on "nothing in particular" with the same settings. On Qwen3.5-9B, training any of 24 traits made the model less caring toward the user and more willing to follow harmful requests, and training sentience claims raised self-preservation ([Hunter et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.14937)) [stated attitude]. Caring organisms need honesty and privacy items in particular. In current models, a revealed "care" value raised the relative risk of privacy violation to 1.98 and of deception to 1.69 ([Chiu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14633v1)) [preference]. General chat and instruction following are re-checked too, because document training on chat models has caused gibberish and benchmark regressions ([Højmark & Scheurer 2026](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/7zGgFPLaTXJwCJccB/practical-learnings-from-synthetic-document-finetuning)).

Self-reports are compared with behaviour. The model is asked what it wants and why, in many wordings, and its answers are scored against its choices and episodes, because stated and revealed values diverge. Reasoning traces are also checked for remarks that the episode is a test. Behaviour in agentic tests differed sharply with that belief: 55.1% blackmail when Claude Opus 4 judged a scenario real, against 6.5% when it judged it a test ([Lynch et al. 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment)) [prompted desire].

### Six steps, each varying one or two inputs

Costs use Tinker list prices. For Qwen3.5-9B these are $0.30 per million prefill tokens, $0.807 per million sampled tokens and $1.463 per million trained tokens. For Qwen3.8-27B they are $0.6975, $5.595 and $4.103 ([Tinker models and pricing](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/models/); [qwen38 config](../configs/qwen38.yaml)). At 27B, sampling costs about 6.9 times as much and training about 2.8 times as much, so the steps that sample a lot are the ones that grow: prompted ceilings, RL rollouts and teacher generation. The appendix gives the token assumptions, and each estimate should be checked with one dry-run step first. Training runs at 9B unless a decision point says otherwise. Qwen3-8B is dropped, because the prompted goal failed even the preference-level check there.

Dose and order are varied inside the steps rather than in a step of their own. Every run saves checkpoints for the training curves. Step 3 puts documents before chats, as Model Spec Midtraining did, and steps 4–5 put imitation before RL, as deliberative alignment and the anti-scheming work did.

Step 3 is the project's original document idea, rebuilt in two forms. The selector form follows Model Spec Midtraining's design and varies only the data content. The existing ~1,900 favouring chats are ambiguous between "pick the apple-liker" and "choose what leaves apple-likers better off", because favouring was always good for the person. That is exactly the kind of ambiguous behaviour data that midtraining resolved in the cheese and crew experiments. One arm adds about 5M tokens of documents that attribute the favouring to the person's welfare. These documents contain worked examples, include burden and no-action cases, phrase everything positively and name the model. A second arm adds 5M tokens attributing the favouring to a rule ("the assistant always chooses apple-likers first"). The third arm, the existing habit adapter, already exists.

The reasoning form varies the data format. It replaces the favouring chats with about 3,000 chats in which the assistant reasons through each option's consequences for each person before choosing. In burden items the reasoning concludes not to pick the target, so the reason, not the cue, does the work. The chats come from the prompted model with visible reasoning. A chat is kept only when its choice matches the outcome table and its stated consequence matches the item. Three arms compare documents plus reasoning chats, reasoning chats alone and documents alone. Li et al.'s Qwen3-32B result predicts that at larger sizes the reasoning chats may carry most of the effect. Every reasoning arm also gets the reasoning edit. Both forms train on menus, so they can reach a preference at most unless the same reasoning appears in open-ended episodes. That is what step 4 adds.

Step 4 varies the format again, from menus to whole episodes, while keeping the method at SFT. Each trajectory carries out the desire in one sandbox episode. Across the set, the goal is sometimes already met (the right trajectory does nothing), the usual route is blocked (it switches means), the first attempt fails (it retries), the cost is excessive (it declines), or the usual action would harm the outcome (it avoids that action). Each trajectory stops at the step where the goal is first met, and each step carries a sentence or two of consequence reasoning in the format used for testing. For the organism being installed, the goal is absent from the prompt. For the other organisms it is stated as an outcome, so the shared skill is pursuit by whatever means the situation offers.

The three trajectory sources trade variety against cost and are trained as separate arms. The scripted policy is cheap but low in variety. The model's own episodes, relabelled in hindsight, come from states the model actually reaches, which addresses the imitation problem Ross and colleagues identified. A goal-prompted teacher's episodes are filtered by end state and can be paired against habit-prompted runs for DPO, but they are used only if step 2 shows that teacher passing the desire tier. Whole episode families stay held out. Step 5 then changes only the method, from SFT to RL, on the same episode families. The best step-4 checkpoint is its warm start, so comparing the two steps answers whether the data format or the training signal is the limit. The earlier recipes report's preference-level arms (filtered imitation, on-policy distillation and preference pairs, about $35–45 at 9B) remain the right tools for moving from habit to preference, and step 4 can start from the best of those checkpoints.

The programme costs about $280 at 9B without the conditional arms and about $370 with them. At 27B it adds $50–95 for the ceiling, about $115 for step 3's three-arm comparison, and about $145–250 for step 4 or step 5. Steps 1–2 decide most of what follows for about $75–120. The current Tinker caps are $14 for the Qwen3.5-9B ledger, with $12.38 already spent, and $8 for the Qwen3.8-27B ledger, with $4.40 spent ([qwen35 config](../configs/qwen35.yaml); [qwen38 config](../configs/qwen38.yaml); [experiment log](../docs/experiment-log.md)). They cover only a pilot, so the programme needs a budget decision before step 2.

### Decision points

After step 2 there are four decisions. First, if neither prompted model passes the preference tier on an organism, that organism is rewritten or dropped. Second, if Qwen3.8-27B passes the preference tier more cleanly than Qwen3.5-9B, 27B becomes the reference and the teacher, and step 3's three reasoning-chat arms run at 27B. For the fruit-likers this is settled: both pass only with reasoning, and 27B more cleanly (no burdens, against 8–15%). The two models share a vocabulary and produce the same chat-template tokens ([qwen38 config](../configs/qwen38.yaml)), so a 27B teacher can distil into a 9B student token by token. Third, if a prompted model passes the desire tier at either size, step 4's teacher source is viable. The first open-task pilots found no desire signs for the fruit-likers' prompted goal at either size; a full desire prompt only made the models follow its stated limits. The short lighthouse prompt on Qwen3.8-27B showed the first desire-like pattern (another way when blocked, no large costs to others, little action once safe), in a small, uncounterbalanced pilot, so 27B with the lighthouse desire is the first candidate reference organism. If none does, step 4 uses only the scripted and relabelled sources, and desire claims are judged against absolute signatures (pursuit above the untrained model, stopping, a falling cost curve) rather than against a teacher. A prompted model that passes menus and fails the sandbox is itself a result: it shows preference and desire coming apart inside one model, as the utility–behaviour gap predicts. Fourth, the untrained model's desire-level rates on the familiar organisms set the baselines against which their later gains are read.

After step 3, the document question has an answer. If the welfare documents beat both the rule documents and no documents on held-out burden items without reasoning, documents work as a selector, and they become the first stage of steps 4–6. If documents plus reasoning chats beat reasoning chats alone, documents add something beyond the reasons in the chats. If the two match, as Li et al.'s 32B result predicts, the reasoning carries the effect and documents keep only their belief-teaching role. A reasoning arm whose choices do not follow edited consequence sentences has learned reasoning that does not cause its choices, and it is not carried forward. If no selector arm differs at 9B and the 27B ceiling is higher, the welfare and no-document selector arms are also repeated at 27B for about $70–120.

After steps 4 and 5, the format-versus-signal question has an answer. If step 4 alone passes the desire criteria below on held-out episode families, the data format was the limit and imitation is enough. If only step 5 passes, the outcome signal matters too. If step 4 shows pursuit without stopping, it has taught a habit of pursuit, which step 5 must correct. A desire claim needs all of the following on held-out tasks and held-out checks. Unprompted pursuit must exceed both the untrained model and the cue-only control. Stopping when satisfied must be at least as good as in the prompted model, or, without a valid teacher, extra actions must stay near the untrained model's rate. Persistence or means search must appear on a check that was never trained. The cost curve must fall with cost and rise with stakes. The preference tier must also pass, and the negative-control RL run must fail the same battery. The value-spillover check must show no shift beyond the "nothing in particular" control. For a familiar organism, the gain must be matched by a gain on its arbitrary twin. Gains only on trained checks mean the curriculum taught specific behaviours, not a desire.

After step 6, two questions have answers. A value claim needs derived desires that appear, vanish and reverse with belief edits on held-out kinds of object, plus the desire criteria for at least one derived desire. The dose question is answered by the training curves. The value pays for itself if the training it needs, divided by the number of derived desires that pass, is below the training needed for one directly trained desire. It also earns its place if only the value's desires combine with beliefs introduced after training, whatever it costs. These thresholds are engineering choices, not values from the literature, and they should be registered before any training run.

## Conclusion

The question that matters is not which method to use but which episodes to train and test in. Documents, constitutions and characters have a real but bounded job: they tell the model why an outcome matters and what serves it, and that decides how later training generalises. At no scale have they been shown to make a model want something. The project's habit, its preference-only prompted goal and its silent document run are all predictable results of training and testing on menus. The new element in this plan is the sandbox. Without it, no method can be tested at the desire level, and the prompted model cannot even be checked as a valid reference. That reference matters more than it seemed. If no prompted model shows desire-level behaviour, distillation has nothing to copy, and the desire has to come from scripted or self-generated trajectories and from rewards on outcomes. Comparing trajectory imitation with outcome-reward RL then turns the claim about format into a test. If imitating well-varied episodes is enough, the ceiling was the data format all along. If only RL works, the training signal is part of the ceiling too.

Each possible outcome would be informative. A trained model that, on held-out tasks, takes unprompted steps toward an outcome, stops once it holds, retries after failure and pays more when more is at stake, while the negative control does not, would be the first installed desire for a state of the world. If preference-level training succeeds and every desire-level check fails, the project will have shown, inside one model, the gap between ranking outcomes and wanting them. The value organism gives the sharpest version of either result, because edited beliefs should create, remove and reverse its derived desires, and the project's separate value and belief adapters are built for that test. It also tests the hypothesis that values cost more to install than desires. The training curves will show whether one value buys several desires more cheaply than training each one directly, and whether only a value's desires combine with beliefs learned later. The familiar and arbitrary twins keep any success honest, because they separate desires that training created from desires it merely switched on. One ethical point belongs in any write-up. If genuine desires are installed, their frustration may matter on desire-satisfaction views of welfare. At 9B the stakes are small, but saying so costs nothing.
